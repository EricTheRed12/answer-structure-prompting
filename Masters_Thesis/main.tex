\subsection{Hook about why language models are great}

With the rise models like ChatGpt and Gemini, large language models (LLMs) have 
shown effectiveness at a variety of natural language tasks. Task that range from solving difficult problems and answering questions on wide range of topics to composing novels. In some cases, LLMs have been so effective it is believed larger models have surpassed humans in certain areas of reasoning and thought.

\subsection{The Problem Statement}

The capabilities demonstrated by large language models are not free. There is a well-documented relationship between the number of model parameters and performance across general tasks --- commonly referred to as scaling laws \cite{kaplan2020scaling}. As model size increases, so does performance on reasoning, question answering, and language generation benchmarks. The result is an industry-wide arms race toward ever-larger models: systems with hundreds of billions of parameters that require significant computational infrastructure to train and deploy \cite{strubell2019energy, patterson2021carbon}.

This creates a fundamental access problem. The models that perform best are available only to organizations with the resources to run them, leaving smaller models as the practical option for the majority of researchers, developers, and end users. However, smaller models carry a well-known trade-off: reduced generalization. A model with fewer parameters has less capacity to encode the breadth of knowledge and reasoning patterns required to perform well across a wide range of tasks \cite{kaplan2020scaling, hoffmann2022chinchilla}.

Compounding this is the problem of hallucinations --- outputs that are factually incorrect or logically inconsistent, produced with apparent confidence \cite{ji2023hallucination, zhang2023hallucination}. Hallucinations are not limited to small models; they affect state-of-the-art systems as well. But they are more pronounced in models with limited parameters, where the probability space over possible responses is less constrained by robust world knowledge. The core issue is that a language model, regardless of size, operates as a probability engine: it generates the most likely next token given its inputs and its learned distributions \cite{vaswani2017attention}. Without sufficient context or capacity, that probability estimate is more easily led astray.

The question this paper is concerned with is whether it is possible to improve the accuracy and generalization of small language models without simply adding more parameters. Specifically: can a structured, generative approach to prompt context --- one that guides a model to identify the type and shape of an answer before generating it --- reduce hallucinations and improve task performance in low-parameter settings?

\subsection{Existing Solutions}

Several techniques have been proposed to improve LLM accuracy and reduce hallucinations without scaling the model itself. Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting \cite{wei2022cot} encourages models to produce intermediate reasoning steps before arriving at an answer, which has been shown to improve performance on multi-step reasoning tasks. Extensions of this idea --- such as Tree of Thoughts \cite{yao2023tot} and Self-Consistency \cite{wang2023selfconsistency} --- further improve outcomes by exploring multiple reasoning paths and selecting the most consistent result.

Few-shot and multi-shot prompting \cite{brown2020gpt3} improve model performance by providing representative examples alongside the query, effectively conditioning the model's output distribution on relevant patterns without modifying its weights. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) \cite{lewis2020rag} takes a different approach, augmenting a model's parametric knowledge with dynamically retrieved external information to ground its responses in verifiable context.

At the architecture level, Mixture of Experts (MoE) \cite{shazeer2017moe, fedus2022switchtransformer} improves effective model capacity by routing inputs to specialized sub-networks, allowing a model to develop targeted competencies across different domains.

Each of these methods addresses a different facet of the problem. CoT and its variants improve the reasoning process but depend on the model already having the relevant knowledge encoded in its parameters. RAG supplies missing knowledge but does not inherently guide the model on how to structure its retrieval or use the retrieved information. MoE improves specialization but operates at training time and requires substantial infrastructure investment.

Critically, what these methods share is a focus on \textit{what} the model knows. They improve accuracy by enriching the model's knowledge, broadening its reasoning paths, or specializing its parameters. What they do not address is \textit{how} the model structures its approach to a problem before generating an answer --- that is, whether the model identifies the type of reasoning required and the shape of the expected response before committing to a generation path. This gap is the central motivation for the framework proposed in this paper.

\subsection{The purpose of the paper}

The purpose of this paper is to present the foundations of a framework that improves the efficacy of small parameter large language modes on a general set of tasks by training the model to identify the correct structure of a response before generating said response. The proposed technique aims to reduce the total set of probable responses while simultaneously providing a structure for the retrieval augmented n-shot prompting of an LLM. As this paper is foundational, the problem set will only cover certain tasks, multiple choice problem solving (fact finding), and analogical reasoning tasks. We will explore the application of this technique on various models and sizes, including both unbiased and pretrained for each task.
