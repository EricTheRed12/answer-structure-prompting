\begin{abstract}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Small LLMs are capable but underutilized due to unguided generation
  \item Existing prompting techniques address \textit{what} the model knows, not \textit{how} it structures its approach
  \item We propose a two-component pipeline: encoder classifier $\rightarrow$ few-shot scaffold $\rightarrow$ small LLM
  \item Central hypothesis: supplying a small LLM with an answer structure matched to the question improves its accuracy, narrowing the gap with larger models
  \item Enabling assumption: a finite taxonomy of answer structure types exists and is learnable by a classifier
  \item Experimental scope: multiple choice QA; evaluated across model sizes and configurations

\end{itemize}

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Hook}

From technology to art and everything in between, large language models have reshaped how we solve problems across every domain. Yet this success comes with a cost in scale and resources that leaves development and deployment in the hands of very few \cite{eloundou2023gpts, strubell2019energy}. Smaller alternatives do exist, but what they provide in accessibility they lose in ability, especially across general tasks \cite{smalllmsready2024, slm_survey2024}. The conventional explanation is the difference in scale. With fewer parameters, smaller models are limited in what they can reliably produce \cite{kaplan2020scaling, costofdownscaling2023}. Nevertheless, their language abilities are largely preserved \cite{linguistic_competence_scale2025}, so the question is: what is it exactly that smaller models lack?

\begin{itemize}
  %\item Large language models have transformed how we solve problems across domains, but their capabilities are bound to scale --- the strongest models are enormous, costly to run, and out of reach for most \cite{eloundou2023gpts, strubell2019energy}n the hands of very few. \cite{eloundou2023gpts, strubell2019energy}
  %\item Small, open models are accessible to nearly anyone, yet they trail their larger counterparts on general tasks, leaving much of their promise unrealized \cite{smalllmsready2024, slm_survey2024}
  %\item The usual explanation is scale --- with fewer parameters, they retain less of what they learn \cite{kaplan2020scaling, costofdownscaling2023}
  %\item Yet their language ability is largely preserved \cite{linguistic_competence_scale2025} --- so is limited capacity really the whole story?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Problem Statement}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Large language models are a transformative technology --- reshaping how humans interact with information, create, and solve problems \cite{eloundou2023gpts, llm_critical_societal2024, llmoverview2023}
  \item But they are not open --- the most capable models are large, closed-source, and accessible only through proprietary APIs \cite{liesenfeld2024closed}
  \item Concentrated control limits who can contribute new ideas and perspectives to the field \cite{kapoor2024societal}
  \item It also determines what those models learn and how they act --- with no external input on training decisions \cite{bommasani2023fmti, liesenfeld2024closed}
  \item Closed-source models cannot be meaningfully audited, understood, or regulated by external parties \cite{bommasani2023fmti}
  \item And even then, many models marketed as open-source aren't truly open --- they provide weights for inference but not the data or infrastructure needed to train them \cite{liesenfeld2024openwashing, liesenfeld2024closed}
  \item Large models carry disproportionate environmental cost in training and inference \cite{strubell2019energy, patterson2021carbon}
  \item Only well-resourced organizations can build or deploy them at scale \cite{strubell2019energy, slm_survey2024}
  \item These constraints are inherent to large models and cannot be resolved through openness alone --- unfortunately, smaller models carry their own limitations
  \item Small open-source models exist but underperform due to limited parametric capacity \cite{modelsizematters2024, kaplan2020scaling}
  \item Small models' lower parameter counts impose a hard cap on retained knowledge and connections \cite{kaplan2020scaling}
  \item Small models cannot encode relational structure for every concept they were trained on --- they retain fragments but lack the capacity to organize all of them \cite{costofdownscaling2023}
  \item This limited capacity manifests as brittleness outside the training distribution and degraded performance on multi-step reasoning and complex real-world queries \cite{costofdownscaling2023}
  \item Despite these limitations, both large and small models share a common ability to understand language \cite{linguistic_competence_scale2025, emergent_reducedscale2024, costofdownscaling2023}
  % NOTE: linguistic_competence_scale2025 finds model size tied to memorization more than core language ability; emergent_reducedscale2024 shows small generative models retain emergent abilities; costofdownscaling2023 shows 60-70% reduction preserves in-context processing. Together these strongly support this claim for decoder LLMs specifically.
  \item The deficit in small models is not linguistic competence but associative depth --- they understand language but lack the structural scaffolding to organize what they do retain \cite{linguistic_competence_scale2025, costofdownscaling2023}
  \item And unlike their larger counterparts, small models are accessible enough for individual researchers to train and modify \cite{slm_survey2024}
  \item Small models can be trained effectively on specific domains, but this specialization comes at the cost of general capability --- a model trained for one domain cannot easily transfer to another \cite{domain_expert_smalllm2024, catastrophic_forgetting2023}
  \item Even so, small models still underperform on general tasks --- numerous approaches exist to address this, including prompt engineering, retrieval augmentation, and architectural specialization; we review them in detail in Section 2
  \item These approaches address knowledge, reasoning paths, or weight adaptation --- expanding what the model knows through retrieval, exploring multiple reasoning paths, or reshaping the weights through specific training --- but none provide a framework for how to structure the response
  \item We hypothesize that external answer structure may allow small models to approach the effectiveness of their larger counterparts
  \item \textbf{Research question:} can external answer structure compensate for limited capacity, making small open-source models effective enough to narrow the gap?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Purpose of the Paper}

\begin{itemize}
  \item This paper proposes and evaluates a framework that supplies small language models with external answer structure at inference time, testing whether it can narrow the gap identified in the problem statement
  \item \textbf{Central hypothesis:} conditioning a small LLM on an answer structure matched to the question improves its accuracy enough to narrow the gap with larger models. This is the claim the experiments are designed to test
  \item We propose a two-component inference-time pipeline requiring no weight updates to the generator:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Component 1:} an encoder-based classifier (BERT-style) maps a question $\rightarrow$ its answer structure type \cite{devlin2019bert}
      \item \textbf{Component 2:} any small LLM generates the answer conditioned on the retrieved few-shot scaffold corresponding to that structure type
    \end{itemize}
  \item The generator can be any small model that understands English \cite{smalllmsready2024, slm_survey2024}
  \item \textbf{Core mechanism:} the scaffold supplies the \textit{dimensions that matter} for a given question, not the answer itself --- the model fills them using its in-context learning ability, which withstands down-scaling even as parametric fact recall declines, drawing on the relational fragments it retains or retrieves \cite{costofdownscaling2023}
  \item The scaffold narrows the probability space \textit{before} generation begins, turning an unguided generation problem into a constrained one --- few in-context examples sharply reduce the model's uncertainty over the latent task \cite{xie2022icl}
  \item \textbf{Operating premises (assumed, not proven):}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item a finite set of answer structure types is sufficient to cover a given task --- we assume such a set exists and construct one rather than proving it \cite{nonfactoid_taxonomy2022}
      \item the answer structure is determined by the semantic context of the question and can be inferred from the question alone --- establishing this question-to-structure mapping in general is beyond the scope of this paper \cite{liroth2002questions}
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Scope:} we test the framework on multiple choice QA only, as a proof of concept; we hypothesize --- but do not claim to demonstrate --- that both the taxonomy and the approach extend to the general question space, which we leave to future work
  \item Evaluation: compare baseline small LLM vs.\ scaffolded small LLM across multiple model sizes and configurations, including base and instruction-tuned variants
\end{itemize}

\section{Literature Review}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Large Language Models}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Large language models are deep neural networks built on the transformer architecture, trained on massive text corpora \cite{vaswani2017attention, llmoverview2023}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Self-attention allows every token to attend to every other token in the sequence --- this is what enables the model to condition on provided context (including prompts and scaffolds) \cite{vaswani2017attention}
      \item ``Large'' refers to parameter count: the learned weights that encode statistical patterns from training, typically hundreds of millions to hundreds of billions
      \item The model learns by adjusting parameters to minimize prediction error across the training corpus --- what it encodes is bounded by its capacity to fit that corpus
    \end{itemize}
  \item Their function is probabilistic token prediction: given a sequence, the model outputs a probability distribution over the next token --- they are prediction engines, not knowledge databases \cite{brown2020gpt3, llmoverview2023}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item The final layer produces a probability distribution over the full vocabulary --- every possible next token gets a likelihood
      \item The model does not ``decide'' an answer --- it assigns likelihoods to all possible continuations based on patterns absorbed during training
      \item Output is inherently stochastic: the same input can produce different outputs, and nothing in the architecture guarantees correctness or consistency
    \end{itemize}
  \item The pre-training objective determines what the model learns to do --- and separates the two architectures relevant to this paper \cite{devlin2019bert, brown2020gpt3}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Decoders (GPT-style): trained via next-token prediction --- see only left context, learn to generate \cite{brown2020gpt3}
      \item Encoders (BERT-style): trained via masked language modeling --- see full context bidirectionally, learn to represent \cite{devlin2019bert}
      \item This paper uses both: a decoder as the generator (small LLM) and an encoder as the classifier (answer structure type prediction)
    \end{itemize}
  \item Decoder LLMs generate via autoregressive decoding: one token at a time, each conditioned only on what came before --- no lookahead, no backtracking, no revision \cite{brown2020gpt3, exposure_bias_ilp2022}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Once a token is emitted, it becomes part of the context for all subsequent tokens --- early commitments propagate forward
      \item If the model begins generating in the wrong direction, it has limited mechanism for self-correction under standard decoding
      \item This is confirmed architecture for all open models\footnote{Closed-source models may employ internal techniques (e.g.\ beam search, speculative decoding, hidden chain-of-thought) that partially mitigate this. However, the small open models this paper targets are confirmed autoregressive decoders \cite{llama3_2024, liesenfeld2024closed}.}
      \item This is the core vulnerability of autoregressive generation: early token commitments constrain all subsequent generation with no opportunity to revise
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Key implication:} the model is a probability engine --- anything that improves output quality does so by shaping the probability distribution over next tokens \cite{prompt_control_theory2023, llmreasoning_stochastic2024}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Scale and Structure}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Scale, as used in the literature, refers primarily to the number of learnable parameters in the model. It is the dominant variable in scaling law research and the conventional predictor of general performance \cite{kaplan2020scaling}
  \item Larger models consistently outperform smaller ones on general tasks. This is well-established in the literature, formalized as scaling laws, and confirmed in industry deployment \cite{kaplan2020scaling, hoffmann2022chinchilla}
  \item The conventional explanation for this advantage is capacity \cite{kaplan2020scaling}. More parameters allow more connections between concepts to be encoded, and with more connections, more ideas can be held together during generation\footnote{Other factors contribute to the performance gap between model sizes, including training data distribution, optimization procedure, and architectural improvements. Training data can be identical across model sizes, so data alone does not explain the gap. Architectural improvements may also contribute, but since many frontier models are closed-source, their internal mechanisms cannot be verified externally \cite{liesenfeld2024closed}. The capacity argument remains central in the literature and is the focus of this section.} \cite{llmoverview2023}
  \item The jump from GPT-2 (1.5B parameters) to GPT-3 (175B parameters) demonstrated this dramatically: GPT-3 performed few-shot tasks that GPT-2 could not perform at all, without any change to architecture or training objective \cite{brown2020gpt3}
  \item Despite this, even at large scale, models produce unexpected output on tasks requiring multi-step logic or structured responses. This is well-documented and systematic \cite{math_reasoning_limits2024, lrm_complexity2025}
  \item More parameters does not mean every valid connection or context is encoded. Scale increases coverage but cannot guarantee completeness for an arbitrary task \cite{kaplan2020scaling, costofdownscaling2023}
  \item If the gap were purely a matter of capacity, external context should not close it. Yet on specific tasks and domains, small models match or exceed large ones when given appropriate context or specialization. The gap is conditional, not absolute \cite{smalllmsready2024, modelsizematters2024}
  \item Small models are not architecturally distinct from large models. They share the same training objective, generation mechanism, and target distributions. What differs is the dimensionality available to represent those distributions; scale determines the resolution of the approximation, not its kind \cite{godey2024softmax, costofdownscaling2023}
  % NOTE: REVISIT — review wording on next pass. This claim establishes architectural equivalence so §2.6 can reference it. Verify godey2024softmax supports "same distributions, fewer dimensions" framing specifically.
  \item We argue that part of the large-model advantage is structural. Larger models have the capacity to encode patterns for how answers should be structured, not just what they should contain. They have seen enough training examples to statistically represent answer structure within their parameters
  % NOTE: REVISIT — need supporting citations for the claim that large models encode answer structure specifically. Current argument is author synthesis only. Find papers showing format/structure sensitivity in large models or evidence of structural priors in pretrained weights.
  \item Because this structure is statistically encoded, it is never deterministic. There is no guarantee the correct structural pattern activates for a given input, even in large models \cite{math_reasoning_limits2024, lrm_complexity2025}
  \item External structure in the input partially compensates for what is not reliably encoded in parameters. Chain of Thought prompting imposes step-by-step structure in the input and measurably improves reasoning performance \cite{wei2022cot}
  \item Brevity constraints demonstrate it more directly: when output form is constrained, small models outperform large ones on mathematical reasoning and scientific knowledge by 7--16 percentage points \cite{brevity_constraints2025}
  \item Neither CoT nor brevity constraints are matched to the specific question. The gap remains: no existing technique classifies the question and provides answer structure matched to it. The following sections review the major approaches that have been tried --- weight modification (\S2.3), prompt engineering (\S2.4), and retrieval augmentation (\S2.5) --- before returning to the mechanistic argument for why structure specifically is what is missing (\S2.6)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Fine-Tuning and Parameter-Efficient Adaptation}

\begin{itemize}
  \item The most established approach to improving model performance is weight modification: update parameters so the model's baseline distribution better fits the desired output \cite{peft_survey2024}
  \item Full fine-tuning updates all weights on task-specific data; parameter-efficient methods (LoRA, adapters, prefix tuning) update a small subset of parameters \cite{hu2022lora, houlsby2019adapters, li2021prefixtuning}. All modify the model's learned distribution
  \item Instruction tuning deserves particular attention: it fine-tunes on instruction-following data and is often framed as improving generalization \cite{instructiontuning2022}. However, instruction-following is itself a task distribution. The model narrows its prediction space toward instruction-formatted responses; this is interpreted as generalization only because the target distribution spans many topics. The mechanism is the same as any fine-tuning: parameters shift to favor one distribution \cite{alignment_tax2024}
  \item A related but distinct approach is knowledge distillation, which trains a smaller model on a larger model's outputs rather than modifying an existing model's weights \cite{hinton2015distilling}. The teacher produces higher-quality outputs because it has more capacity, and the student learns from those outputs rather than raw training data. However, the student still operates within its own parametric capacity: the same dimensional bottleneck (see \S2.2) limits how much can be retained at full resolution. Distillation may improve the student relative to its baseline, but it cannot overcome the fundamental capacity constraint; the student holds a lower-resolution approximation of what the teacher provides
  \item Whether modifying existing parameters or distilling new ones, all these approaches narrow the model's probability distribution toward the training task. This results in degraded performance on tasks outside that distribution \cite{catastrophic_forgetting2023, alignment_tax2024}. This is not a side effect but the mechanism itself. For a paper whose goal is to improve general-task performance without sacrificing generality, weight modification is not just insufficient but counterproductive
  \item Moreover, weight modification addresses a problem this paper argues is not the bottleneck. As established in \S2.2, the abilities required for our framework (language understanding, in-context learning) already survive down-scaling \cite{costofdownscaling2023, linguistic_competence_scale2025}
  \item The deficit is parametric: small models lack the capacity to encode answer structure reliably (see \S2.2). However, the solution is not to modify those parameters. Fine-tuning cannot add capacity that is not there, and it narrows what capacity exists. The alternative is to supply externally what the parameters cannot encode. Provide the structure in the input rather than relying on it to emerge from the weights
  % NOTE: "Fine-tuning cannot add capacity" — this is definitionally true: fine-tuning reshapes existing parameters but does not increase hidden dimension or total parameter count. PEFT methods (LoRA etc.) add small auxiliary parameters but do not expand the model's representational bottleneck (the hidden dimension that Godey et al. identify as the constraint). If a reviewer pushes on this, the response is architectural: the softmax bottleneck is a function of hidden dimension, which no fine-tuning method changes.
  % NOTE: §3 should clarify that the framework is compatible with instruction-tuned models — it operates on top of whatever model you have (base or instruction-tuned). The argument here is that fine-tuning alone doesn't solve the problem, not that it's incompatible with our approach.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Prompt Engineering and In-Context Learning}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Prompt engineering is the category of techniques that shape model output at inference time through input construction, rather than weight modification \cite{brown2020gpt3}
  \item Zero-shot prompting provides only the question and task framing, relying on the model's parametric knowledge with minimal contextual constraint. System and instruction prompting (``You are a medical expert,'' ``Answer the following multiple choice question:'') add task-level context \cite{brown2020gpt3, sahoo2024promptsurvey}. Both operate at the task level: the same instruction applies uniformly to all questions regardless of their structural requirements
  \item Few-shot and many-shot prompting provide example input-output pairs in the prompt to condition generation \cite{brown2020gpt3, manyshot2024}
  \item Chain-of-Thought prompting elicits intermediate generation from the model before the final answer \cite{wei2022cot}. CoT provides generic process structure (``think step by step'') that measurably improves multi-step performance in large models, demonstrating that structuring the generation process improves output quality. Variations include Tree of Thoughts, which explores multiple reasoning paths as a branching search \cite{yao2023tot}, and Self-Consistency, which samples multiple chains and selects the most frequent answer \cite{wang2023selfconsistency}
  \item These techniques share a common principle: additional context in the input narrows the prediction space \cite{brown2020gpt3, prompt_control_theory2023}. Zero-shot provides minimal constraint; system prompts add task-level constraint; few-shot examples add example-level constraint; CoT adds self-generated intermediate constraint. Each narrows the space of probable outputs in different ways, and each demonstrably improves model performance
  \item This mechanism is called in-context learning (ICL). The model uses examples or instructions provided in the context window as input that directly influences its output distribution, requiring no weight updates \cite{brown2020gpt3}. Even a few examples sharply reduce uncertainty over what the model should produce \cite{xie2022icl}
  % NOTE: REVISIT — Xie et al. frame ICL as implicit Bayesian inference over the latent task. May want to reintroduce that framing when defining "prediction space" in §2.6 or §2.8.
  \item For small models, where parametric capacity is limited, ICL remains effective because it operates through attention over the context window rather than retrieval from stored parameters \cite{costofdownscaling2023}. Attention is architectural rather than capacity-dependent, making input-side interventions viable regardless of model size
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Retrieval-Augmented Generation}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Retrieval-Augmented Generation is an inference-time system that automates prompt construction by retrieving relevant information from an external corpus and injecting it into the model's input \cite{lewis2020rag, rag_survey2023}
  \item Like prompt engineering (\S2.4), RAG operates on the input side without modifying model weights. The distinction is where the context originates: prompt engineering provides context designed by the prompt author (instructions, examples, elicited reasoning), while RAG provides context retrieved from an external knowledge source indexed at runtime \cite{sahoo2024promptsurvey}
  \item For example, question-decomposition RAG breaks a complex query into sub-questions, retrieves separately for each, and synthesizes the results \cite{question_decomp_rag2025}. By simplifying complex information needs into focused retrieval targets, decomposition provides the model with more precise and relevant content at each step
  \item Corrective RAG evaluates retrieval quality before passing content to the model, rejecting or revising documents that do not meet relevance thresholds \cite{corrective_rag2024}. By filtering what the model is allowed to generate from, these mechanisms prevent low-quality retrievals from degrading output
  \item RAG demonstrates that the principle established in \S2.4 extends further: structuring the input process improves output quality, and doing so with specific, relevant information produces better results than generic structure alone \cite{question_decomp_rag2025, corrective_rag2024}. Organization and specificity together outperform either in isolation
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Unconstrained Prediction}

\begin{itemize}
  \item What is commonly called ``hallucination'' is output that does not match the expected or correct answer for a given task \cite{ji2023hallucination, hallucination_unified2024}
  \item However, at the architectural level, the model's generation process is autoregressive next-token prediction: regardless of internal complexity, every forward pass produces a probability distribution over the next token (see \S2.1). This operation is the same whether the output is task-correct or task-incorrect. We argue that any token the model produces is a statistically probable continuation given its parameters and context, and that there is no separate failure mode that produces hallucinations \cite{brown2020gpt3, llmreasoning_stochastic2024}
  \item The model's parameters determine the baseline distribution over possible outputs. In small models, this baseline is less precise: as established in \S2.2, they approximate the same target distributions as large models but with fewer dimensions. The mechanistic basis for this is a dimensional bottleneck; the target distribution is high-rank, and when a model's hidden dimension falls below what is required to express it, the approximation degrades \cite{godey2024softmax, costofdownscaling2023}
  % NOTE: REVISIT — review wording on next pass. Depends on §2.2 architectural equivalence addition. Verify godey2024softmax supports this framing.
  \item This approximation compounds during autoregressive generation: each token is produced from an imprecise representation of what should come next, and that imprecision propagates forward through the sequence with limited mechanism for self-correction under standard decoding \cite{lpsr2026, exposure_bias_ilp2022}
  \item We argue that this compounding cannot be eliminated from autoregressive systems, but its scope can be reduced by narrowing the space of probable outputs before generation begins. Empirical evidence supports this: constraining output form alone inverts model-size performance rankings on reasoning tasks (see \S2.2) \cite{prompt_control_theory2023, brevity_constraints2025}
  \item The preceding sections reviewed approaches that reduce the prediction space: modifying parameters reshapes the baseline distribution itself (\S2.3), while enriching context through prompting (\S2.4) or retrieval (\S2.5) constrains the distribution at inference time by providing additional signal the model conditions on
  \item Answer structure (defined in \S2.8) reduces the prediction space by providing the output form before generation begins, so that the model's remaining task is to populate it with relevant content. This reduces the probability mass on structurally incorrect continuations regardless of the model's parametric coverage \cite{llmreasoning_stochastic2024}
  % NOTE: May need workshopping later.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Question Classification in QA}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Question type classification is a well-established component of question answering systems, with over two decades of research treating it as a standard preprocessing step. Li and Roth (2002) introduced a hierarchical taxonomy of question types and demonstrated that a learned classifier could assign types with high accuracy from question text alone; subsequent work has extended and refined these taxonomies across domains \cite{liroth2002questions, nonfactoid_taxonomy2022}
  \item Classification is tractable because different question types exhibit distinct, observable features at the surface level. A factual question calls for a single datum as answer; an analogical question calls for a relational mapping between two domains; a multi-step question calls for a sequence of linked conclusions \cite{liroth2002questions, analogical_reasoning2022, wei2022cot}. These structural signals are present in the question text itself, independent of the answer content
  \item Encoder-based classifiers reliably map question text to categorical labels; this is a mature capability demonstrated across multiple taxonomies, with modern BERT-based models achieving state-of-the-art performance on both open-domain and domain-specific question classification benchmarks \cite{liroth2002questions, devlin2019bert, xu2019qc_bert}. The same mechanism applies to any finite label set derived from question features, including novel taxonomies not represented in existing benchmarks. The classification task is structurally identical regardless of label set: assign a question to one of a finite set of categories based on features observable in the text. We argue that the surface features distinguishing question types correlate directly with answer structure requirements --- a ``who'' question signals a proper noun or person as answer; a ``how does X relate to Y'' question signals a relational mapping. The classifier targets a different label set but reads from the same signal space
  \item The QA literature uses question classification to determine expected answer types (person, location, date) for retrieval filtering and candidate ranking \cite{liroth2002questions, nonfactoid_taxonomy2022}. More recent work has explored selecting structured templates based on task or question properties, but none match the specific intervention proposed here:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Han et al.\ (2025) generate reasoning blueprints per task category that guide small models through problem-solving processes \cite{blueprints_slm2025}. \textbf{Similar:} structured templates matched to task type improve SLM performance without weight updates. \textbf{Distinct:} blueprints define a reasoning process (how to approach the problem), not an output form; and they are matched at the task-category level (math, coding, logic) rather than classified from individual question text
      \item Wu et al.\ (2025) construct a problem-solving template library and match templates to problems during RL training \cite{templaterl2025}. \textbf{Similar:} a finite library of structural templates selected per problem. \textbf{Distinct:} templates define reasoning strategy, not answer form; selection occurs during training via reinforcement learning, not at inference time via a trained classifier
      \item Yadavalli (2025) uses an encoder-based classifier to route queries to different response templates based on complexity \cite{dynamic_template_selection2025}. \textbf{Similar:} a trained classifier selects from a finite set of templates before generation. \textbf{Distinct:} the goal is cost optimization (shorter responses for simple queries), not accuracy improvement; templates control verbosity rather than defining the structural form a valid answer must take
    \end{itemize}
  To the best of our knowledge, no existing work classifies individual questions to select a structural template that defines the form a valid answer must take and provides it to a generator as input constraint before generation begins
  \item We argue that the structural correspondence between question types and answer types is not incidental: a question's type determines the structural form its answer must take. If this correspondence is systematic, it is classifiable; and if it is classifiable, it can be made explicit and provided to a model as input constraint rather than left implicit in the model's parameters. The following section develops this hypothesis
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Answer Structure Types: A Taxonomy Hypothesis}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \S2.7 established that questions exhibit distinct, classifiable structural patterns; surface features that determine what type of answer is required. We argue that this structural regularity extends to the answer side: if a question's type determines what form a valid answer must take, then answer forms are themselves a structured, identifiable set. Different question types call for different answer structures
  \item We define an \textit{answer structure} as the set of dimensions a valid answer to a question must address; not the specific facts or values, but what kinds of information must be present for a response to count as complete. It is analogous to a schema: it defines the shape of the output without determining its values. A question's answer structure specifies what components are required; the model's job is to fill them with content
  \item For example, a question asking ``What sport uses X?'' calls for an answer structure with the dimensions: (1) the sport's name, (2) how X is used in the sport, and (3) distinguishing context that confirms the identification. A question asking ``How does A relate to B?'' calls for a different structure: (1) the nature of A, (2) the nature of B, and (3) the relationship between them. A question asking ``Why did X happen?'' calls for yet another: (1) the event itself, (2) the causal factors, and (3) how those factors connect to the outcome. In each case the structure tells the model what to produce, not what the answer is; the model fills the dimensions from whatever knowledge its parameters or context supply. These examples are illustrative, not exhaustive; this paper tests the hypothesis on a constrained task domain (\S4)
  % NOTE: §4 (Methodology) forward reference — when §4 is revised, ensure it describes the empirical test of finiteness (Experiment 1: LLM-based answer structure extraction from SQuAD, identifying patterns across filled structures).
  \item We hypothesize that answer structure types are finite, enumerable, and classifiable from question text alone. While questions are generative and infinite in content, the structural forms they take are bounded; the question classification literature demonstrates this on the question side, where finite taxonomies have operated successfully for over two decades \cite{liroth2002questions}. Bolotova et al.\ demonstrate that non-factoid questions can be classified into a finite set of types defined by what kind of response they require \cite{nonfactoid_taxonomy2022}. We argue that if the kind of response a question requires is classifiable, the structural form of that response can be defined and provided to a model as input constraint. If a small model is provided with the correct answer structure before generation begins, its task reduces from general-purpose prediction to populating a known form; this reduction is what allows it to generalize across question types that would otherwise exceed its parametric capacity. The structure supplies what the model's parameters cannot reliably encode (\S2.2), compensating for limited capacity without requiring weight modification (\S2.3)
  \item Answer structure is externally supplied and present in the input before generation begins, counteracting the compounding error problem where self-generated tokens propagate imprecision forward (\S2.6). It is classified per-question rather than applied generically, providing the kind of matched specificity that outperforms uniform approaches (\S2.2, \S2.5). And it constrains at the semantic level; defining what dimensions to produce; rather than relying on the model to activate the correct structural pattern probabilistically from its parameters. One distinction worth noting: unlike few-shot prompting, which provides finished examples and relies on the model to infer the underlying pattern \cite{xie2022icl}, answer structure provides the pattern itself directly. The model does not need to reverse-engineer what the examples have in common; it receives the dimensions to fill and generates the content
\end{itemize}

\section{Architecture}
% ============================================================

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Overview:} two-stage inference-time pipeline, no weight updates required
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Stage 1 --- Classification: encoder model classifies question $\rightarrow$ answer structure type
      \item Stage 2 --- Generation: small LLM generates answer conditioned on retrieved scaffold
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Stage 1 --- The Classifier}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Architecture: BERT-style bidirectional encoder \cite{devlin2019bert}
      \item Input: raw question text
      \item Output: answer structure type label (from the taxonomy)
      \item Training: fine-tuned on labeled question-type dataset \cite{datasets_survey2024}
      \item Why an encoder: bidirectional attention gives richer question representations than a left-to-right decoder; classification is a natural fit \cite{devlin2019bert, liu2019roberta}
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The Taxonomy}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Define the set of answer structure types used in this paper (e.g.\ factual-recall, analogical, multi-step reasoning, multiple-choice selection)
      \item Discuss how the taxonomy was derived
      \item Justify that the taxonomy is principled, not arbitrary \cite{nonfactoid_taxonomy2022}
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Stage 2 --- Scaffold Retrieval}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Each taxonomy class has a corresponding pre-constructed few-shot prompt scaffold
      \item Scaffold structure: [answer type description] + [N worked examples] + [question]
      \item The scaffold narrows the probability space before generation begins \cite{brown2020gpt3, manyshot2024}
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Stage 2 --- The Generator}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Any small, English-understanding LLM (baseline or instruction-tuned)
      \item No fine-tuning required
      \item The scaffold does the structural work; the model supplies language understanding \cite{smalllmsready2024}
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Why this works (theoretical argument):}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item LLMs are probability engines; the scaffold shifts the prior toward valid response structures
      \item This is analogous to how few-shot prompting works, but at the structural level rather than the topical level \cite{llmreasoning_stochastic2024, brown2020gpt3}
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Related routing concept --- Mixture of Experts (MoE):} MoE divides a model into specialized sub-networks routed by a learned gate \cite{shazeer2017moe, fedus2022switchtransformer}. Conceptually similar to our classifier-based routing: both select a specialized pathway based on the input. However, MoE operates at training time within the model architecture and requires massive infrastructure; our classifier operates at inference time with no retraining of the generator \cite{moe_survey2024}
\end{itemize}

\section{Methodology}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Research questions:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item RQ1: Does structural scaffolding improve small LLM accuracy on multiple choice QA?
      \item RQ2: Is the improvement consistent across different model sizes?
      \item RQ3: Does the classifier reliably identify answer structure types?
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Dataset selection}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Multiple choice QA benchmarks (e.g.\ ARC, MMLU, HellaSwag) --- \needpaper{} ARC and MMLU dataset citations
      \item Justify: clear correctness criteria, well-established benchmarks with diverse question types
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Models evaluated}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Range of small LLMs across parameter counts (e.g.\ 125M, 350M, 1B, 3B)
      \item Both base and instruction-tuned variants
      \item Specific models TBD based on availability
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Experimental conditions}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Baseline:} small LLM, zero-shot, no scaffold, no retrieval
      \item \textbf{Structure only:} small LLM + classifier-generated structural scaffold, no retrieval
      \item \textbf{RAG only:} small LLM + retrieved context, no structural scaffold
      \item \textbf{Structure + RAG:} small LLM + classifier-generated structural scaffold + retrieved context (structure guides both generation and retrieval)
      \item \textbf{Ablation --- random scaffold:} small LLM + random scaffold (wrong structure type) --- tests whether classification quality matters or scaffolding volume alone drives improvement
      \item \textbf{Oracle upper bound:} small LLM + correct scaffold manually assigned --- sets the ceiling for a perfect classifier
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What the 2$\times$2 design tests:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Structure-only vs.\ baseline: does structure alone improve performance?
      \item Structure+RAG vs.\ RAG-only: does structure improve RAG-augmented generation?
      \item Structure+RAG vs.\ structure-only: does RAG add value on top of structure?
      \item If structure improves performance in both the with-RAG and without-RAG conditions, it validates that the structural intervention is independently beneficial regardless of knowledge augmentation
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Classifier training}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Training data: labeled question-type examples
      \item Architecture: fine-tuned BERT / RoBERTa \cite{devlin2019bert, liu2019roberta}
      \item Evaluation: accuracy of structure type prediction
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Evaluation metrics}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Primary: accuracy on multiple choice questions
      \item Secondary: classifier accuracy on answer structure type prediction
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
