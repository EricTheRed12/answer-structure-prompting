\begin{abstract}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Small LLMs are capable but underutilized due to unguided generation
  \item Existing prompting techniques address \textit{what} the model knows, not \textit{how} it structures its approach
  \item We propose a two-component pipeline: encoder classifier $\rightarrow$ few-shot scaffold $\rightarrow$ small LLM
  \item Central hypothesis: supplying a small LLM with an answer structure matched to the question improves its accuracy, narrowing the gap with larger models
  \item Enabling assumption: a finite taxonomy of answer structure types exists and is learnable by a classifier
  \item Experimental scope: multiple choice QA; evaluated across model sizes and configurations

\subsection{Hook}
% NOTE: FINAL DRAFT — consider merging §1.1 and §1.2 into a single flowing Introduction. Read back-to-back after prose rewrite and trim overlap if repetition feels heavy.

From technology to art and everything in between, large language models have reshaped how we solve problems across every domain. Yet this success comes with a cost in scale and resources that leaves development and deployment in the hands of very few \cite{eloundou2023gpts, strubell2019energy}. Smaller alternatives do exist, but what they provide in accessibility they lose in ability, especially across general tasks \cite{smalllmsready2024, slm_survey2024}. The conventional explanation is the difference in scale. With fewer parameters, smaller models are limited in what they can reliably produce \cite{kaplan2020scaling, costofdownscaling2023}. Nevertheless, their language abilities are largely preserved \cite{linguistic_competence_scale2025}, so the question is: what is it exactly that smaller models lack?

\subsection{The Problem Statement}

Large language models represent a fundamental shift in how humans interact with information \cite{eloundou2023gpts, llm_critical_societal2024, llmoverview2023}. Despite this, the largest and most capable models are closed-source and accessible only through heavily controlled commercial APIs \cite{liesenfeld2024closed}. Due to their size and the resources required to produce even one of these models, few organizations have the ability to effectively develop and deploy them \cite{strubell2019energy, slm_survey2024}. As a result, this level of control hinders progress in the broader research community \cite{kapoor2024societal}.

Open-source models offer an alternative, though even these have their faults. The largest still require specialized hardware to operate, further limiting what is accessible \cite{slm_survey2024}. The smaller models fare even worse. Scaling laws dictate that as model size decreases, so too does effectiveness \cite{kaplan2020scaling}. Fewer parameters impose a hard limit on retained knowledge and on a model's ability to encode the relational structure between concepts \cite{kaplan2020scaling, costofdownscaling2023}. In practice, this manifests as brittleness outside training distributions, degraded multi-step reasoning, and reduced performance on complex queries \cite{costofdownscaling2023}.

Despite these limitations, large language models of all sizes share one common aspect: they exhibit a general linguistic competence \cite{linguistic_competence_scale2025, emergent_reducedscale2024, costofdownscaling2023}. Related work has shown that smaller models can be effective on specific tasks \cite{smalllmsready2024, modelsizematters2024}, and that approaches like fine-tuning and prompt engineering can measurably increase performance \cite{hu2022lora, wei2022cot}, but neither overcomes the limits of parametric capacity \cite{kaplan2020scaling, costofdownscaling2023}. The question becomes, if models share a similar linguistic competence and can be fine-tuned for specific domains, how can we make the most accessible models better across all tasks? This paper explores the idea that generalization can be increased by providing a structured prompt representing the key aspects of an answer.

\subsection{The Purpose of the Paper}

The hypothesis is straightforward. By providing a model with a structured prompt that specifies the possible dimensions of an answer, the model's prediction space is narrowed \cite{prompt_control_theory2023}. We hypothesize that this results in more accurate output. This can be applied to any given task provided the answer structure has already been identified. Thus, we propose a two-component inference-time pipeline: a classifier to identify the answer structure, and a generator model that takes the structure as an input. The classifier is encoder-based (BERT-style) \cite{devlin2019bert} and classifies a question into a taxonomy of predefined answer structures. The generator can be any decoder-style LLM. It is designed to be model-agnostic, provided the model understands English \cite{smalllmsready2024}.

This paper operates under two key premises. First, a finite set of common answer dimensions exists and can cover a given domain \cite{nonfactoid_taxonomy2022}. Second, the structure that holds these dimensions can be recognized from the question alone \cite{liroth2002questions}. We construct a taxonomy from the questions in the SQuAD dataset to verify that it can be created and then classified. This paper does not aim to extend this taxonomy beyond a limited domain, nor to establish a definitive taxonomy, only to demonstrate that the concept has merit.

We evaluate this taxonomy across a variety of generator models, including various sizes under 15B parameters, in both base and fine-tuned variants. We measure success by the performance of each generator on the questions in the SQuAD dataset against its own baseline results.

\subsection{Large Language Models}

Large language models are a class of deep neural networks built on the transformer architecture and trained on massive corpora of text \cite{vaswani2017attention, llmoverview2023}. The transformer encodes tokens, discrete units representing words or subwords. Through its self-attention mechanism, every token attends to every other token in the sequence \cite{vaswani2017attention}, allowing the model to encode the structural features of language. These include the relationships between words and phrases, syntax, and semantics. The same mechanism allows the model to condition on provided context, including prompts and scaffolds supplied at inference time \cite{brown2020gpt3}. Parametric capacity is the key constraint on what a model can encode from the training corpus \cite{kaplan2020scaling}, but regardless of size, the fundamental mechanism remains \cite{costofdownscaling2023}.

By design, a large language model is a prediction engine. It does not store exact or factual information, but the statistical relationships between tokens learned during training. When a model receives a prompt, it performs a forward pass that produces a probability distribution over the full vocabulary. Every possible continuation receives a likelihood. The output has no guarantee of correctness. It is simply the most statistically probable continuation given the input and the model's learned parameters. Furthermore, the model produces an identical distribution for a given input every time. Any difference in output depends on the sampling configuration. The result is a prediction shaped entirely by the input it receives.

Once generation begins, each selected token becomes the input for the next prediction, narrowing the output distribution further \cite{brown2020gpt3}. A wrong prediction at any point can alter the trajectory of the entire response \cite{exposure_bias_ilp2022, lpsr2026}. This is the nature of autoregressive decoding: the model generates one token at a time, each conditioned only on what came before, with no mechanism for lookahead or self-correction \cite{brown2020gpt3, exposure_bias_ilp2022}. The quality of the output is therefore contingent on the initial prompt \cite{prompt_control_theory2023}. We acknowledge this may not hold for all large language models, but this is the confirmed architecture of every open model evaluated in this paper.\footnote{Closed-source models may employ internal techniques that partially mitigate autoregressive limitations, but their architectures cannot be independently verified \cite{liesenfeld2024closed}.}

What the model receives as input directly determines the distribution from which tokens are selected.

\subsection{Scale and Structure}

Scale, as used in the literature, refers to the number of parameters in a language model \cite{kaplan2020scaling}. It is the dominant predictor of general performance. Larger models consistently outperform their smaller counterparts across a wide range of domains \cite{kaplan2020scaling, hoffmann2022chinchilla}. The prevailing explanation attributes this to capacity: more parameters encode a higher-resolution approximation of the training distribution, preserving relationships that smaller models are forced to compress or discard \cite{llmoverview2023, godey2024softmax}.

This can be observed in Brown et al. \cite{brown2020gpt3}. A GPT-3 model with 175B parameters executed few-shot tasks that GPT-3 at 1.3B parameters was incapable of performing. There were no changes to the architecture or training regime. The only variable was the number of parameters.

Scale alone, however, does not fully explain the difference. Even the largest models fail on multi-step reasoning tasks and produce hallucinations \cite{math_reasoning_limits2024, lrm_complexity2025}. The gap is conditional, not absolute. On specific tasks in specific domains, small models given appropriate context can match and even exceed large ones \cite{smalllmsready2024, modelsizematters2024}.

Architecturally, small models are not distinct from larger ones. They share the same generation mechanisms and are trained on the same corpora toward the same target distribution \cite{godey2024softmax, costofdownscaling2023}. The difference in scale, then, must be the resolution of the approximation stored in the parameters.

We argue that the advantage provided by scale is structural. The capacity of larger models allows them to encode the same structures at higher resolution \cite{godey2024softmax}. Consider an image file: reduce the pixels and the image remains, but in a degraded form. Reduce it too far and the image breaks down entirely. In this analogy, the image represents the structural patterns of language. These include how to format a response, how to relate concepts across a sequence, and how to organize an answer to match the type of question being asked. Yet unlike a deterministic structure such as a JSON schema, in an LLM these patterns are statistically encoded and non-deterministic, resulting in possible corruption regardless of model size \cite{math_reasoning_limits2024, lrm_complexity2025}.

Building on this, prompting techniques are effective because they compensate for degraded structural encoding \cite{prompt_control_theory2023}. Techniques like brevity constraints demonstrate this directly. Constraining output form alone shifts performance hierarchies by up to 26 percentage points, demonstrating that structure, not just capacity, determines output quality \cite{brevity_constraints2025}.

Brevity demonstrates the principle, but prompting alone does not solve generalization. For a small model to generalize, it will need a direct way to compensate for the parametric constraint.

\subsection{Fine-Tuning and Parameter-Efficient Adaptation}

Often the first step to improving a model is affecting its weights \cite{peft_survey2024}. The logic is simple: the parameters store encoded information, and by updating this encoding to better match a given task, the model should improve.

Full fine-tuning updates every parameter; adapter methods insert small trainable modules between existing layers; prefix tuning prepends learnable tokens to the input \cite{hu2022lora, houlsby2019adapters, li2021prefixtuning}. Some, like LoRA, update smaller subsets of parameters to reduce computational cost \cite{hu2022lora}. Each updates a model's learned distribution by reshaping its weights on task-specific data.

Instruction tuning deserves particular attention. This method of fine-tuning relies on instruction-following data to teach the model how to respond to a variety of task prompts \cite{instructiontuning2022}. The result can easily be interpreted as generalization. The tuning narrows the model's prediction space over a target distribution that spans multiple topics. Yet instruction-following is itself a task distribution, not a general capability. The underlying mechanism remains: the model's parameters shift to favor a specific distribution \cite{alignment_tax2024}.

A related approach worth highlighting is knowledge distillation. This process trains a smaller model using a larger model's outputs, updating the student's weights with the teacher's output encodings \cite{hinton2015distilling}. As the teacher has a higher capacity for encoding, its outputs are of higher quality, resulting in more focused training than general fine-tuning. The student model, however, is still limited in capacity. No matter how focused the training outputs are, the encoding still loses resolution due to the same dimensional bottleneck (\S2.2) \cite{godey2024softmax, costofdownscaling2023}. Distillation may improve performance relative to a baseline, but it cannot overcome fundamental capacity constraints.

Fine-tuning, we argue, does not solve the generalization problem in smaller models. As established in \S2.2, the abilities required for generalization, language understanding and in-context learning, have been shown to survive downscaling \cite{costofdownscaling2023, linguistic_competence_scale2025}.

Modifying a model's existing parameters or distilling new ones can even be detrimental to generalization. The changes narrow a model's probability distribution toward the specific training task \cite{catastrophic_forgetting2023, alignment_tax2024}, and fine-tuning for one task results in degraded performance on others. When the objective is general-task performance, weight modification is not just insufficient but counterproductive.

Fine-tuning cannot add capacity that is not there, and it narrows what capacity exists. The alternative is to supply what the parameters cannot reliably encode at inference time.

% NOTE: "Fine-tuning cannot add capacity" — this is definitionally true: fine-tuning reshapes existing parameters but does not increase hidden dimension or total parameter count. PEFT methods (LoRA etc.) add small auxiliary parameters but do not expand the model's representational bottleneck (the hidden dimension that Godey et al. identify as the constraint). If a reviewer pushes on this, the response is architectural: the softmax bottleneck is a function of hidden dimension, which no fine-tuning method changes.

\subsection{Prompt Engineering and In-Context Learning}

Prompt engineering shapes model output by constructing the input at inference time. The additional context narrows the prediction space, resulting in more accurate output \cite{brown2020gpt3, prompt_control_theory2023}. These techniques do not update model parameters and are agnostic to model version.
% NOTE: REVIEW — alternative/additional citations for the mechanistic claim about token relationships as signal:
% - "Unveiling the Dynamics of Generative Transformers" (arXiv:2402.13512) — maps self-attention to context-conditioned Markov chains; shows input tokens condition output distribution through attention
% - "Interpreting Context Look-ups in Transformers" (arXiv:2402.15055) — identifies attention heads that recognize relevant context tokens and activate associated next-token neurons

Several variations of prompting techniques exist. Zero-shot, for example, is the most basic, providing only the initial question and a framing of the task \cite{brown2020gpt3}. This relies heavily on the model's parametric knowledge with minimal contextual constraint. Few-shot techniques provide more context in the form of example input-output pairs. Providing these concrete patterns to the model helps constrain both the format and content of its response \cite{brown2020gpt3, sahoo2024promptsurvey}. Techniques like chain-of-thought provide explicit context related to the processing of the prompt, directing the model to reason through intermediate steps before producing an answer \cite{wei2022cot}. The common thread is that all these techniques provide additional context to the input. In addition to activating more of the model's encoded relationships, the explicit structure between tokens in the prompt becomes additional signal the model can attend over \cite{olsson2022inductionheads}, narrowing the prediction space \cite{prompt_control_theory2023}. This mechanism is known as in-context learning (ICL) \cite{brown2020gpt3}.

ICL operates through a model's attention rather than its parametric capacity, and the structure it provides is external to the model, allowing it to function when capacity is limited. This makes ICL scale-resistant \cite{costofdownscaling2023}. As an input-side intervention, ICL can also be applied across a variety of domains and models without prior training or modification \cite{brown2020gpt3}. Few-shot prompting, for instance, does not prescribe what the examples must be, only that examples improve performance \cite{sahoo2024promptsurvey}. The prompt structure remains constant while the content varies with the task.

This does not mean prompt engineering alone can solve the problem of generalization at a small scale. The existing techniques work well, but still require the model to produce outputs based on information encoded in its parameters. When the model has fewer parameters, that information suffers the same resolution loss established in \S2.2.

Chain-of-thought prompting highlights this explicitly. It works well on larger models \cite{wei2022cot} but suffers significant degradation on smaller ones \cite{cot_degradation_slm2025, smallmodel_learnability2025}. Even with the additional structure provided by the prompt, iteratively generating on an incorrect encoding causes the prediction space to expand \cite{exposure_bias_ilp2022, lpsr2026}.

Prompt engineering effectively narrows the prediction space, but still cannot entirely account for the parametric capacity gap. One takeaway, however, is that a prompt with specific information, like the few-shot technique, offers a path worth exploring.

\subsection{Retrieval-Augmented Generation}

% --- AUTHOR: Write each claim as one paragraph below. Original bullets preserved in comments above. ---

Retrieval-Augmented Generation, as the name implies, augments generation by retrieving additional context to construct the prompt at inference time \cite{lewis2020rag, rag_survey2023}. This retrieval pulls information from an external source.

Like prompt engineering (\S2.4), RAG uses in-context learning to improve a model's performance without modifying its internal weights \cite{lewis2020rag}. The difference is in what each provides. Prompt engineering supplies structure and framing designed by the author to activate relationships the model might not otherwise prioritize. RAG supplies specific content retrieved per-query to cover information the model's parameters may not encode. The two are not mutually exclusive; RAG can augment prompt engineering and vice versa \cite{prompt_eng_rag_slm2026}.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation's value is in specificity. Retrieval from an external source supplies explicitly task-relevant tokens at inference time \cite{lewis2020rag}. These can range from factual content pulled from a static knowledge base to passages selected dynamically based on query similarity \cite{rag_survey2023}. As established in \S2.4, the mechanism that allows this to improve performance is the increased number of relevant tokens in the prompt \cite{brown2020gpt3, prompt_control_theory2023}. This additional context increases the implicit relational structure during generation reducing the prediction space. Certain RAG techniques provide organization beyond just relevant context. For example, question-decomposition approaches break a complex query into sub-questions and retrieve separately for each, providing more precise content at each step and a clear breakdown of a given problem \cite{question_decomp_rag2025}. As with prompting (\S2.4), more context improves performance, more context with structure improves performance further, and context with both structure and specificity outperforms either alone.

As an inference-time technique, RAG is both generalizable and scale-resistant. A retrieval system can be applied to any model, trained or untrained, and depending on the technique, applied across a variety of domains without modification \cite{rag_survey2023}. Its core mechanism operates through attention rather than parametric capacity, meaning benefits persist when models are smaller \cite{costofdownscaling2023}. However, the same limitation identified in prompt engineering (\S2.4) applies: there remains a reliance on the model's encoded information to organize its output. To reduce the prediction space further, a prompt needs to provide everything but the answer.

When a model does not produce the expected or correct answer for a given task, it is common to call the error a hallucination \cite{ji2023hallucination, hallucination_unified2024}. At the architectural level, this is a misleading description. The model did not make a mistake nor imagine a false reality. It simply produced an output that did not match the task. As introduced in \S2.1, for every forward pass the model produces a probability distribution derived entirely from the prompt \cite{brown2020gpt3, llmreasoning_stochastic2024}. There is no alternative failure mode.

LLMs use temperature to reshape the softmax probability curve and a random seed to select from that distribution, creating the appearance of variety \cite{temperature_impact2025}. Like the neural networks they are built on, if these values are static, LLMs will always produce the same output. When these values vary, they produce different but adjacent outputs. Seed determines which token is drawn from the distribution; strong relationships are preserved, so divergence is minimal \cite{seed_temperature_stability2025}. Temperature reshapes the distribution itself. Selecting adjacent tokens in the probability space can cause the output to diverge more significantly \cite{temperature_impact2025}. Neither actively improves the generation.

As established in \S2.1, the input defines the trajectory of the prediction, which we argue makes it the only point of intervention \cite{prompt_control_theory2023}. If the quality of the input determines the quality of the output, then it is critical the input has the right context. Chain-of-thought demonstrates what happens when this context is absent. When the input does not sufficiently constrain the trajectory, the model generates purely from its internal representations. If those representations do not match the task space, the output diverges significantly \cite{cot_degradation_slm2025, smallmodel_learnability2025}.

Larger models often have this specificity and structure encoded in their parameters. Smaller models lack this resolution (\S2.2). Prompt engineering can supply structure (\S2.4), but the structure it provides is generic and is applied uniformly regardless of the task. RAG supplies specificity (\S2.5), but not in a form adapted to the specific task. Even combined, no existing technique provides structure adapted to the individual task. To surpass the parametric barrier and enable generalization, what is needed is a structure that is task-independent in mechanism, specific to the task in its dimensions, and consistent in form.

\subsection{Question Classification in QA}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Question type classification is a well-established component of question answering systems, with over two decades of research treating it as a standard preprocessing step. Li and Roth (2002) introduced a hierarchical taxonomy of question types and demonstrated that a learned classifier could assign types with high accuracy from question text alone; subsequent work has extended and refined these taxonomies across domains \cite{liroth2002questions, nonfactoid_taxonomy2022}
  \item Classification is tractable because different question types exhibit distinct, observable features at the surface level. A factual question calls for a single datum as answer; an analogical question calls for a relational mapping between two domains; a multi-step question calls for a sequence of linked conclusions \cite{liroth2002questions, analogical_reasoning2022, wei2022cot}. These structural signals are present in the question text itself, independent of the answer content
  \item Encoder-based classifiers reliably map question text to categorical labels; this is a mature capability demonstrated across multiple taxonomies, with modern BERT-based models achieving state-of-the-art performance on both open-domain and domain-specific question classification benchmarks \cite{liroth2002questions, devlin2019bert, xu2019qc_bert}. The same mechanism applies to any finite label set derived from question features, including novel taxonomies not represented in existing benchmarks. The classification task is structurally identical regardless of label set: assign a question to one of a finite set of categories based on features observable in the text. We argue that the surface features distinguishing question types correlate directly with answer structure requirements --- a ``who'' question signals a proper noun or person as answer; a ``how does X relate to Y'' question signals a relational mapping. The classifier targets a different label set but reads from the same signal space
  \item The QA literature uses question classification to determine expected answer types (person, location, date) for retrieval filtering and candidate ranking \cite{liroth2002questions, nonfactoid_taxonomy2022}. More recent work has explored selecting structured templates based on task or question properties, but none match the specific intervention proposed here:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Han et al.\ (2025) generate reasoning blueprints per task category that guide small models through problem-solving processes \cite{blueprints_slm2025}. \textbf{Similar:} structured templates matched to task type improve SLM performance without weight updates. \textbf{Distinct:} blueprints define a reasoning process (how to approach the problem), not an output form; and they are matched at the task-category level (math, coding, logic) rather than classified from individual question text
      \item Wu et al.\ (2025) construct a problem-solving template library and match templates to problems during RL training \cite{templaterl2025}. \textbf{Similar:} a finite library of structural templates selected per problem. \textbf{Distinct:} templates define reasoning strategy, not answer form; selection occurs during training via reinforcement learning, not at inference time via a trained classifier
      \item Yadavalli (2025) uses an encoder-based classifier to route queries to different response templates based on complexity \cite{dynamic_template_selection2025}. \textbf{Similar:} a trained classifier selects from a finite set of templates before generation. \textbf{Distinct:} the goal is cost optimization (shorter responses for simple queries), not accuracy improvement; templates control verbosity rather than defining the structural form a valid answer must take
    \end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \S2.7 established that questions exhibit distinct, classifiable structural patterns; surface features that determine what type of answer is required. We argue that this structural regularity extends to the answer side: if a question's type determines what form a valid answer must take, then answer forms are themselves a structured, identifiable set. Different question types call for different answer structures
  \item We define an \textit{answer structure} as the set of dimensions a valid answer to a question must address; not the specific facts or values, but what kinds of information must be present for a response to count as complete. It is analogous to a schema: it defines the shape of the output without determining its values. A question's answer structure specifies what components are required; the model's job is to fill them with content
  \item For example, a question asking ``What sport uses X?'' calls for an answer structure with the dimensions: (1) the sport's name, (2) how X is used in the sport, and (3) distinguishing context that confirms the identification. A question asking ``How does A relate to B?'' calls for a different structure: (1) the nature of A, (2) the nature of B, and (3) the relationship between them. A question asking ``Why did X happen?'' calls for yet another: (1) the event itself, (2) the causal factors, and (3) how those factors connect to the outcome. In each case the structure tells the model what to produce, not what the answer is; the model fills the dimensions from whatever knowledge its parameters or context supply. These examples are illustrative, not exhaustive; this paper tests the hypothesis on a constrained task domain (\S4)
  % NOTE: §4 (Methodology) forward reference — when §4 is revised, ensure it describes the empirical test of finiteness (Experiment 1: LLM-based answer structure extraction from SQuAD, identifying patterns across filled structures).
  \item We hypothesize that answer structure types are finite, enumerable, and classifiable from question text alone. While questions are generative and infinite in content, the structural forms they take are bounded; the question classification literature demonstrates this on the question side, where finite taxonomies have operated successfully for over two decades \cite{liroth2002questions}. Bolotova et al.\ demonstrate that non-factoid questions can be classified into a finite set of types defined by what kind of response they require \cite{nonfactoid_taxonomy2022}. We argue that if the kind of response a question requires is classifiable, the structural form of that response can be defined and provided to a model as input constraint. If a small model is provided with the correct answer structure before generation begins, its task reduces from general-purpose prediction to populating a known form; this reduction is what allows it to generalize across question types that would otherwise exceed its parametric capacity. The structure supplies what the model's parameters cannot reliably encode (\S2.2), compensating for limited capacity without requiring weight modification (\S2.3)
  \item Answer structure is externally supplied and present in the input before generation begins, counteracting the compounding error problem where self-generated tokens propagate imprecision forward (\S2.6). It is classified per-question rather than applied generically, providing the kind of matched specificity that outperforms uniform approaches (\S2.2, \S2.5). And it constrains at the semantic level; defining what dimensions to produce; rather than relying on the model to activate the correct structural pattern probabilistically from its parameters. One distinction worth noting: unlike few-shot prompting, which provides finished examples and relies on the model to infer the underlying pattern \cite{xie2022icl}, answer structure provides the pattern itself directly. The model does not need to reverse-engineer what the examples have in common; it receives the dimensions to fill and generates the content
\end{itemize}

\section{Architecture}
% ============================================================

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Overview:} two-stage inference-time pipeline, no weight updates required
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Stage 1 --- Classification: encoder model classifies question $\rightarrow$ answer structure type
      \item Stage 2 --- Generation: small LLM generates answer conditioned on retrieved scaffold
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Stage 1 --- The Classifier}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Architecture: BERT-style bidirectional encoder \cite{devlin2019bert}
      \item Input: raw question text
      \item Output: answer structure type label (from the taxonomy)
      \item Training: fine-tuned on labeled question-type dataset \cite{datasets_survey2024}
      \item Why an encoder: bidirectional attention gives richer question representations than a left-to-right decoder; classification is a natural fit \cite{devlin2019bert, liu2019roberta}
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The Taxonomy}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Define the set of answer structure types used in this paper (e.g.\ factual-recall, analogical, multi-step reasoning, multiple-choice selection)
      \item Discuss how the taxonomy was derived
      \item Justify that the taxonomy is principled, not arbitrary \cite{nonfactoid_taxonomy2022}
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Stage 2 --- Scaffold Retrieval}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Each taxonomy class has a corresponding pre-constructed few-shot prompt scaffold
      \item Scaffold structure: [answer type description] + [N worked examples] + [question]
      \item The scaffold narrows the probability space before generation begins \cite{brown2020gpt3, manyshot2024}
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Stage 2 --- The Generator}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Any small, English-understanding LLM (baseline or instruction-tuned)
      \item No fine-tuning required
      \item The scaffold does the structural work; the model supplies language understanding \cite{smalllmsready2024}
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Why this works (theoretical argument):}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item LLMs are probability engines; the scaffold shifts the prior toward valid response structures
      \item This is analogous to how few-shot prompting works, but at the structural level rather than the topical level \cite{llmreasoning_stochastic2024, brown2020gpt3}
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Related routing concept --- Mixture of Experts (MoE):} MoE divides a model into specialized sub-networks routed by a learned gate \cite{shazeer2017moe, fedus2022switchtransformer}. Conceptually similar to our classifier-based routing: both select a specialized pathway based on the input. However, MoE operates at training time within the model architecture and requires massive infrastructure; our classifier operates at inference time with no retraining of the generator \cite{moe_survey2024}
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Research questions:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item RQ1: Does structural scaffolding improve small LLM accuracy on multiple choice QA?
      \item RQ2: Is the improvement consistent across different model sizes?
      \item RQ3: Does the classifier reliably identify answer structure types?
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Dataset selection}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Multiple choice QA benchmarks (e.g.\ ARC, MMLU, HellaSwag) --- \needpaper{} ARC and MMLU dataset citations
      \item Justify: clear correctness criteria, well-established benchmarks with diverse question types
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Models evaluated}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Range of small LLMs across parameter counts (e.g.\ 125M, 350M, 1B, 3B)
      \item Both base and instruction-tuned variants
      \item Specific models TBD based on availability
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Experimental conditions}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Baseline:} small LLM, zero-shot, no scaffold, no retrieval
      \item \textbf{Structure only:} small LLM + classifier-generated structural scaffold, no retrieval
      \item \textbf{RAG only:} small LLM + retrieved context, no structural scaffold
      \item \textbf{Structure + RAG:} small LLM + classifier-generated structural scaffold + retrieved context (structure guides both generation and retrieval)
      \item \textbf{Ablation --- random scaffold:} small LLM + random scaffold (wrong structure type) --- tests whether classification quality matters or scaffolding volume alone drives improvement
      \item \textbf{Oracle upper bound:} small LLM + correct scaffold manually assigned --- sets the ceiling for a perfect classifier
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What the 2$\times$2 design tests:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Structure-only vs.\ baseline: does structure alone improve performance?
      \item Structure+RAG vs.\ RAG-only: does structure improve RAG-augmented generation?
      \item Structure+RAG vs.\ structure-only: does RAG add value on top of structure?
      \item If structure improves performance in both the with-RAG and without-RAG conditions, it validates that the structural intervention is independently beneficial regardless of knowledge augmentation
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Classifier training}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Training data: labeled question-type examples
      \item Architecture: fine-tuned BERT / RoBERTa \cite{devlin2019bert, liu2019roberta}
      \item Evaluation: accuracy of structure type prediction
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Evaluation metrics}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Primary: accuracy on multiple choice questions
      \item Secondary: classifier accuracy on answer structure type prediction
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
