\begin{abstract}
Small language models are limited by capacity, and prompting is one way to compensate at inference time. This work investigates whether supplying a small model with an answer structure matched to the question improves its accuracy on extractive question answering. The premise is that such a structure narrows the prediction space. A taxonomy of answer structures was derived from SQuAD. Six models between 1B and 8B were then evaluated with the matched structure, a mismatched structure, the source passage, and their combinations. The structure did not improve accuracy and often reduced it, whereas the passage improved every model. A matched structure did, however, consistently outperform a mismatched one, indicating that the taxonomy captures a real distinction the model responds to. The failure therefore lies not in the taxonomy but in its delivery: an explicit schema competes with the structure a model already reads rather than reinforcing it. Structure matters to a small model, but an imposed schema is not an effective way to supply it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Hook}
% NOTE: FINAL DRAFT — consider merging §1.1 and §1.2 into a single flowing Introduction. Read back-to-back after prose rewrite and trim overlap if repetition feels heavy.

From technology to art and everything in between, large language models have reshaped how we solve problems across every domain. Yet this success comes with a cost in scale and resources that leaves development and deployment in the hands of very few \cite{eloundou2023gpts, strubell2019energy}. Smaller alternatives do exist, but what they provide in accessibility they lose in ability, especially across general tasks \cite{smalllmsready2024, slm_survey2024}. The conventional explanation is the difference in scale. With fewer parameters, smaller models are limited in what they can reliably produce \cite{kaplan2020scaling, costofdownscaling2023}. Nevertheless, their language abilities are largely preserved \cite{linguistic_competence_scale2025}, so the question is: what is it exactly that smaller models lack?

\subsection{The Problem Statement}

Large language models represent a fundamental shift in how humans interact with information \cite{eloundou2023gpts, llm_critical_societal2024, llmoverview2023}. Despite this, the largest and most capable models are closed-source and accessible only through heavily controlled commercial APIs \cite{liesenfeld2024closed}. Due to their size and the resources required to produce even one of these models, few organizations have the ability to effectively develop and deploy them \cite{strubell2019energy, slm_survey2024}. As a result, this level of control hinders progress in the broader research community \cite{kapoor2024societal}.

Open-source models offer an alternative, though even these have their faults. The largest still require specialized hardware to operate, further limiting what is accessible \cite{slm_survey2024}. The smaller models fare even worse. Scaling laws dictate that as model size decreases, so too does effectiveness \cite{kaplan2020scaling}. With fewer parameters, a model retains less of the knowledge encoded during training \cite{costofdownscaling2023}. In practice, this manifests as brittleness outside training distributions, degraded multi-step reasoning, and reduced performance on complex queries \cite{costofdownscaling2023}.

Despite these limitations, large language models of all sizes share one common aspect: they exhibit a general linguistic competence \cite{linguistic_competence_scale2025, emergent_reducedscale2024, costofdownscaling2023}. Related work has shown that smaller models can be effective on specific tasks \cite{smalllmsready2024, modelsizematters2024}, and that approaches like fine-tuning and prompt engineering can measurably increase performance \cite{hu2022lora, wei2022cot}, but neither overcomes the limits of parametric capacity \cite{kaplan2020scaling, costofdownscaling2023}. The question becomes, if models share a similar linguistic competence and can be fine-tuned for specific domains, how can we make the most accessible models better across all tasks? This paper explores the idea that generalization can be increased by providing a structured prompt representing the key aspects of an answer.

\subsection{The Purpose of the Paper}

The hypothesis is straightforward. By providing a model with a structured prompt that specifies the possible dimensions of an answer, the model's prediction space is narrowed. We hypothesize that this narrowing results in more accurate output. This can be applied to any given task provided the answer structure has already been identified. Thus, we propose a two-component inference-time pipeline: a classifier to identify the answer structure, and a generator model that takes the structure as an input. The classifier is encoder-based (BERT-style) \cite{devlin2019bert} and classifies a question into a taxonomy of predefined answer structures. The generator can be any decoder-style LLM. It is designed to be model-agnostic, provided the model understands English \cite{smalllmsready2024}.

This paper operates under two key premises. First, a finite set of common answer dimensions exists and can cover a given domain \cite{nonfactoid_taxonomy2022}. Second, the structure that holds these dimensions can be recognized from the question alone \cite{liroth2002questions}. We construct a taxonomy from the questions in the SQuAD dataset to verify that it can be created and then classified. This paper does not aim to extend this taxonomy beyond a limited domain, nor to establish a definitive taxonomy, only to demonstrate that the concept has merit.

We evaluate this taxonomy across a variety of generator models at various sizes in base pretrained form. We measure success by the performance of each generator on the questions in the SQuAD dataset against its own baseline results.

\section{Literature Review}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Large Language Models}

Large language models are a class of deep neural networks built on the transformer architecture and trained on massive corpora of text \cite{vaswani2017attention, llmoverview2023}. The transformer encodes tokens, discrete units representing words or subwords. Through its self-attention mechanism, every token attends to every other token in the sequence \cite{vaswani2017attention}, allowing the model to encode the structural features of language. These include the relationships between words and phrases, syntax, and semantics. The same mechanism allows the model to condition on provided context, including prompts and scaffolds supplied at inference time \cite{brown2020gpt3}. Parametric capacity is the key constraint on what a model can encode from the training corpus \cite{kaplan2020scaling}, but regardless of size, the fundamental mechanism remains \cite{costofdownscaling2023}.

By design, a large language model is a prediction engine. It does not store exact or factual information, but the statistical relationships between tokens learned during training. When a model receives a prompt, it performs a forward pass that produces a probability distribution over the full vocabulary. Every possible continuation receives a likelihood. The output has no guarantee of correctness. It is simply the most statistically probable continuation given the input and the model's learned parameters. Furthermore, the model produces an identical distribution for a given input every time. Any difference in output depends on the sampling configuration. The result is a prediction shaped entirely by the input it receives.

Once generation begins, each selected token becomes the input for the next prediction, narrowing the output distribution further \cite{brown2020gpt3}. A wrong prediction at any point can alter the trajectory of the entire response \cite{exposure_bias_ilp2022, lpsr2026}. This is the nature of autoregressive decoding: the model generates one token at a time, each conditioned only on what came before, with no mechanism for lookahead or self-correction \cite{brown2020gpt3, exposure_bias_ilp2022}. The quality of the output is therefore contingent on the initial prompt \cite{prompt_control_theory2023}. We acknowledge this may not hold for all large language models, but this is the confirmed architecture of every open model evaluated in this paper.\footnote{Closed-source models may employ internal techniques that partially mitigate autoregressive limitations, but their architectures cannot be independently verified \cite{liesenfeld2024closed}.}

What the model receives as input directly determines the distribution from which tokens are selected.

\subsection{Scale and Structure}

Scale, as used in the literature, refers to the number of parameters in a language model \cite{kaplan2020scaling}. It is the dominant predictor of general performance. Larger models consistently outperform their smaller counterparts across a wide range of domains \cite{kaplan2020scaling, hoffmann2022chinchilla}. The prevailing explanation attributes this to capacity: a larger parameter count encodes more of the information in the training data, and smaller models are forced to compress or discard a portion of it \cite{llmoverview2023, knowledgecapacity2024}.

This can be observed in Brown et al. \cite{brown2020gpt3}. A GPT-3 model with 175B parameters executed few-shot tasks that GPT-3 at 1.3B parameters was incapable of performing. There were no changes to the architecture or training regime. The only variable was the number of parameters.

Scale alone, however, does not fully explain the difference. Even the largest models fail on multi-step reasoning tasks and produce hallucinations \cite{math_reasoning_limits2024, lrm_complexity2025}. The gap is conditional, not absolute. On specific tasks in specific domains, small models given appropriate context can match and even exceed large ones \cite{smalllmsready2024, modelsizematters2024}.

Architecturally, small models are not distinct from larger ones. They share the same generation mechanisms and are trained on the same corpora toward the same target distribution \cite{godey2024softmax, costofdownscaling2023}. The difference in scale, then, must be the resolution of the approximation stored in the parameters.

We argue that the advantage provided by scale is structural. The capacity of larger models allows them to encode the same structures at higher resolution \cite{godey2024softmax}. Consider an image file: reduce the pixels and the image remains, but in a degraded form. Reduce it too far and the image breaks down entirely. In this analogy, the image represents the structural patterns of language. These include how to format a response, how to relate concepts across a sequence, and how to organize an answer to match the type of question being asked. Yet unlike a deterministic structure such as a JSON schema, in an LLM these patterns are statistically encoded and non-deterministic, resulting in possible corruption regardless of model size \cite{math_reasoning_limits2024, lrm_complexity2025}.

Building on this, we argue that prompting techniques are effective because they compensate for degraded structural encoding. Supplying a small model with a structured, task-matched guide at inference time improves its performance without any change to its weights \cite{han2025blueprints}. This demonstrates that structure, in addition to capacity, can have an impact on output quality.

Prompting alone does not solve generalization. For a small model to generalize, it will need a direct way to compensate for the parametric constraint.

\subsection{Fine-Tuning and Parameter-Efficient Adaptation}

Often the first step to improving a model is affecting its weights \cite{peft_survey2024}. The logic is simple: the parameters store encoded information, and by updating this encoding to better match a given task, the model should improve.

Full fine-tuning updates every parameter; adapter methods insert small trainable modules between existing layers; prefix tuning prepends learnable tokens to the input \cite{hu2022lora, houlsby2019adapters, li2021prefixtuning}. Some, like LoRA, update smaller subsets of parameters to reduce computational cost \cite{hu2022lora}. Each updates a model's learned distribution by reshaping its weights on task-specific data.

Instruction tuning deserves particular attention. This method of fine-tuning relies on instruction-following data to teach the model how to respond to a variety of task prompts, and has been shown to improve generalization to tasks unseen during tuning \cite{instructiontuning2022}. This can easily be interpreted as general capability. We argue instead that instruction-following is itself a task distribution: the tuning narrows the model's prediction space over a target distribution that happens to span many topics. As instruction-tuning data grows, performance on knowledge and reasoning benchmarks declines \cite{alignment_tax2024}. The underlying mechanism remains the same, the model's parameters shift to favor a specific distribution.

A related approach worth highlighting is knowledge distillation. This process trains a smaller model using a larger model's outputs, updating the student's weights with the teacher's output encodings \cite{hinton2015distilling}. As the teacher has a higher capacity for encoding, its outputs are of higher quality, resulting in more focused training than general fine-tuning. The student model, however, is still limited in capacity. No matter how focused the training outputs are, the encoding still loses resolution due to the same dimensional bottleneck (\S2.2) \cite{godey2024softmax, costofdownscaling2023}. Distillation may improve performance relative to a baseline, but it cannot overcome fundamental capacity constraints.

Fine-tuning, we argue, does not solve the generalization problem in smaller models. As established in \S2.2, the abilities required for generalization, language understanding and in-context learning, have been shown to survive downscaling \cite{costofdownscaling2023, linguistic_competence_scale2025}.

Modifying a model's existing parameters or distilling new ones carries a further risk. Fine-tuning narrows a model's probability distribution toward the specific training task, which can degrade performance on others \cite{catastrophic_forgetting2023}. When the objective is general-task performance, this is a tradeoff rather than a clear gain. Weight modification can sharpen a model on one distribution while eroding the breadth that general-task performance depends on.

Fine-tuning cannot add capacity that is not there, and it narrows what capacity exists. An alternative is to supply what the parameters cannot reliably encode at inference time.


\subsection{Prompt Engineering and In-Context Learning}

Prompt engineering shapes model output by constructing the input at inference time. Additional context aligned with the input query can narrow the model's prediction space, reducing output error \cite{whendoescontexthelp2026}. These techniques do not update model parameters and are agnostic to model version.

Several variations of prompting techniques exist. Zero-shot, for example, is the most basic, providing only the initial question and a framing of the task \cite{brown2020gpt3}. This relies heavily on the model's parametric knowledge with minimal contextual constraint. Few-shot techniques provide more context in the form of example input-output pairs. Providing these concrete patterns to the model helps constrain both the format and content of its response \cite{brown2020gpt3, sahoo2024promptsurvey}. Techniques like chain-of-thought provide explicit context related to the processing of the prompt, directing the model to reason through intermediate steps before producing an answer \cite{wei2022cot}. The common thread is that all these techniques provide additional context to the input. In addition to activating more of the model's encoded relationships, the explicit structure between tokens in the prompt becomes additional signal the model can attend over \cite{olsson2022inductionheads}, narrowing the prediction space. This mechanism is known as in-context learning (ICL) \cite{brown2020gpt3}.

ICL operates through a model's attention rather than its parametric capacity, and the structure it provides is external to the model, allowing it to function when capacity is limited. This makes ICL scale-resistant \cite{costofdownscaling2023}. As an input-side intervention, ICL can also be applied across a variety of domains and models without prior training or modification \cite{brown2020gpt3}. Few-shot prompting, for instance, does not prescribe what the examples must be, only that examples improve performance \cite{sahoo2024promptsurvey}. The prompt structure remains constant while the content varies with the task.

This does not mean prompt engineering alone can solve the problem of generalization at a small scale. The existing techniques work well, but still require the model to produce outputs based on information encoded in its parameters. When the model has fewer parameters, that information suffers the same resolution loss established in \S2.2.

Chain-of-thought prompting highlights this explicitly. It works well on larger models \cite{wei2022cot} but suffers significant degradation on smaller ones \cite{cot_degradation_slm2025, smallmodel_learnability2025}. Even with the additional structure provided by the prompt, iteratively generating on an incorrect encoding causes the prediction space to expand \cite{exposure_bias_ilp2022, lpsr2026}.

Prompt engineering effectively narrows the prediction space, but still cannot entirely account for the parametric capacity gap. One takeaway, however, is that a prompt with specific information, like the few-shot technique, offers a path worth exploring.


\subsection{Retrieval-Augmented Generation}


Retrieval-Augmented Generation, as the name implies, augments generation by retrieving additional context to construct the prompt at inference time \cite{lewis2020rag, rag_survey2023}. This retrieval pulls information from an external source.

Like prompt engineering (\S2.4), RAG uses in-context learning to improve a model's performance without modifying its internal weights \cite{lewis2020rag}. The difference is in what each provides. Prompt engineering supplies structure and framing designed by the author to activate relationships the model might not otherwise prioritize. RAG supplies specific content retrieved per-query to cover information the model's parameters may not encode. The two are not mutually exclusive; RAG can augment prompt engineering and vice versa \cite{prompt_eng_rag_slm2026}.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation's value is in specificity. Retrieval from an external source supplies explicitly task-relevant content at inference time \cite{lewis2020rag}. These can range from factual content pulled from a static knowledge base to passages selected dynamically based on query similarity \cite{rag_survey2023}. As established in \S2.4, providing more relevant context in the prompt improves performance \cite{brown2020gpt3}. This additional context increases the implicit relational structure during generation, reducing the prediction space. Certain RAG techniques provide organization beyond just relevant context. For example, question-decomposition approaches break a complex query into sub-questions and retrieve separately for each, providing more precise content at each step and a clear breakdown of a given problem \cite{question_decomp_rag2025}. As with prompting (\S2.4), more context improves performance, more context with structure improves performance further, and context with both structure and specificity outperforms either alone.

As an inference-time technique, RAG is both generalizable and scale-resistant. A retrieval system can be applied to any model, trained or untrained, and depending on the technique, applied across a variety of domains without modification \cite{rag_survey2023}. Its core mechanism operates through attention rather than parametric capacity, meaning benefits persist when models are smaller \cite{costofdownscaling2023}. However, the same limitation identified in prompt engineering (\S2.4) applies: there remains a reliance on the model's encoded information to organize its output. To reduce the prediction space further, a prompt needs to provide everything but the answer.

\subsection{Unconstrained Prediction}


When a model does not produce the expected or correct answer for a given task, it is common to call the error a hallucination \cite{ji2023hallucination, hallucination_unified2024}. At the architectural level, this is a misleading description. The model did not make a mistake nor imagine a false reality. It simply produced an output that did not match the task. As introduced in \S2.1, for every forward pass the model produces a probability distribution derived entirely from the prompt \cite{brown2020gpt3, llmreasoning_stochastic2024}. These errors arise from the ordinary statistical behavior of fitting the training distribution \cite{whyllmshallucinate2024}. There is no alternative failure mode.

LLMs use temperature to reshape the softmax probability curve and a random seed to select from that distribution, creating the appearance of variety \cite{temperature_impact2025}. Like the neural networks they are built on, if these values are static, LLMs will always produce the same output. When these values vary, they produce different but adjacent outputs. Seed determines which token is drawn from the distribution; the shape of that distribution is unchanged, so divergence is bounded \cite{seed_temperature_stability2025}. Temperature reshapes the distribution itself. Selecting adjacent tokens in the probability space can cause the output to diverge more significantly \cite{temperature_impact2025}. Neither actively improves the generation.

As established in \S2.1, the input defines the trajectory of the prediction, which we argue makes it the primary point of intervention. If the quality of the input determines the quality of the output, then it is critical the input has the right context. Chain-of-thought demonstrates what happens when this context is absent. When the input does not sufficiently constrain the trajectory, the model generates purely from its internal representations. If those representations do not match the task space, the output diverges significantly \cite{cot_degradation_slm2025, smallmodel_learnability2025}.

Larger models often have this specificity and structure encoded in their parameters. Smaller models lack this resolution (\S2.2). Prompt engineering can supply structure (\S2.4), but the structure it provides is generic and is applied uniformly regardless of the task. RAG supplies specificity (\S2.5), but not in a form adapted to the specific task. Even combined, no existing technique provides structure adapted to the individual task. To surpass the parametric barrier and enable generalization, what is needed is a structure that is task-independent in mechanism, specific to the task in its dimensions, and consistent in form.

\subsection{Question Classification in QA}


Question classification is well-established in computer science and foundational to question answering systems. Li and Roth demonstrate that a taxonomy of question classes exists and that these classes can be identified from the question text alone \cite{liroth2002questions}. Different classes of questions have different associated features. For example, some are factual, others map relations. Bolotova et al. establish a taxonomy of this kind for non-factoid questions, whose answers are open-ended rather than short spans \cite{nonfactoid_taxonomy2022}. Its class signals are observable at the surface level and even extend across subject domains. If answer structure is finite and identifiable there, it follows that answer structure would be identifiable for the extractive, span-based questions studied here (\S4), whose answers are less complex.

We propose that if questions are classifiable, and every question has a relevant answer, there must be some structure or set of features that can be used to identify groups of answers. We extend the premise that questions are classifiable from their text \cite{liroth2002questions} to argue that the question type shapes the form its answer takes. For instance, a factual question calls for a single fact. Analogical reasoning requires a relational mapping between two subjects \cite{analogical_reasoning2022}, so an analogical question calls for a mapping as its answer. Multi-step problems benefit from a sequence of intermediate conclusions \cite{wei2022cot}, so a multi-step question calls for a sequence. Not all relationships between question type and answer form are equally explicit, but the patterns are learnable from sufficient examples. An encoder model like BERT, which excels at classification, could be fine-tuned to recognize this new taxonomy \cite{devlin2019bert, xu2019qc_bert}.

A taxonomy of answer forms satisfies the requirements established in \S2.6. The relational patterns are content-independent and can be applied across specific answers regardless of task. The dimensions within each form can be explicitly provided to a model before generation begins. The classification produces consistent output regardless of domain. What remains is a clear definition of this form.

\subsection{Answer Structure Types: A Taxonomy Hypothesis}

We define an answer structure as a schema that specifies what a valid answer must contain without specifying what the answer is. Each entry in the schema is a dimension: a required component of the answer. The answer structure defines what kinds of information are needed, how they relate, and how they must be organized. The structure provides explicit relational context in the input, constraining the prediction space before generation begins. When prompted, the model's remaining task is to populate the structure with relevant content.

The following examples illustrate how question types produce distinct answer structures. A relational question such as ``How does A relate to B?'' requires specific dimensions: (1) the nature of A, (2) the nature of B, and (3) how they relate. A causal question such as ``Why did X happen?'' calls for a different set: (1) the event, (2) the preceding causes, and (3) the causal mechanism. A procedural question such as ``How do you do X?'' differs again: (1) the task, (2) the steps required, and (3) the order of the steps. In each case the answer requires subjects, a mechanism of relation, and joining details. The dimensions vary across types, but the structural pattern is consistent. The number of dimensions is not fixed. In all cases, the structure can be identified from the question and provided before generation begins. These examples are illustrative, not exhaustive. This paper tests this hypothesis on a constrained task domain (\S4).

What an answer structure provides to a model is additional context. Answer structures use in-context learning to narrow the prediction space of the generation task (\S2.4). The structure provides dimensional hooks, such as subjects or mechanisms, as input tokens. For example, a prompt containing only ``describe a historical event'' leaves the prediction space broad. Adding dimensional tokens such as ``the event: a maritime disaster, the causes: environmental and navigational, the mechanism: causal chain'' builds explicit relationships between these tokens, narrowing the space of valid continuations. The more input tokens are included, the more attention is constrained. Fewer output tokens receive high probability, and the result is a sharper distribution. For smaller models, the sharpened distribution compensates for limited parametric resolution (\S2.2). The model has more signal to work with and in return produces more accurate output.

Answer structures also enable generalization. As a defined taxonomy, they can be applied across many problems with the same process: classify the question, provide the structure, populate the dimensions. The only change between tasks is the structure itself. Unlike few-shot prompting, which provides examples that the model must infer a pattern from, the answer structure provides a direct mapping to the answer form. Theoretical work suggests a broader principle: a prompt matched to a task's structure outperforms a generic one \cite{taskspecific_prompts2025}. Our taxonomy applies this principle at the level of individual question prompts.

We further hypothesize that the space answer structures operate in is bounded. While a structure need not come from a finite taxonomy to be effective, there is a finite set of structures that can be identified. This stems from the classification of questions, bound at the highest level by the six interrogatives: who, what, when, where, why, and how. At any level below, subtypes can be defined with as much or as little granularity as required, forming a static taxonomy \cite{liroth2002questions, nonfactoid_taxonomy2022}. Once predefined, this enables automation through classification. Questions can be infinite, but as the interrogatives show, content is infinite while form is bounded.

\subsection{Related Work}
% ============================================================

Several recent works cover adjacent problems, and each contributes an element of our approach.

SMART proposes that properties of the answer can be identified from the input question alone \cite{setty2023smart, smart2021}. This is done by predicting the ontological entity type an expected answer requires. The predicted type is used to narrow the retrieval search space so that only entities of that type are considered. This work demonstrates a question contains enough signal to identify features of an answer before producing one.

Dynamic Template Selection classifies incoming prompts by semantic complexity to select a pre-built response template \cite{yadavalli2025dts}. The templates determine the output's verbosity and structure. The objective is to reduce the cost of generation through more efficient token usage. For our purposes, this work highlights that classification of an input can map directly to a templated taxonomy.

TemplateRL constructs a library of problem-solving templates \cite{wu2025templaterl}. Like our answer structures, these templates provide structured context to assist in generation. The difference is that TemplateRL's templates provide a set of reasoning action sequences, indexed by problem complexity and retrievable per-problem. This work establishes that a structured library can be maintained and retrieved on a per-prompt basis to improve generation.

The Blueprints devised by Han et al. structure step-by-step reasoning to guide generation \cite{han2025blueprints}. By providing these as input tokens to a small model, they are shown to increase performance. No weight updates are required; the intervention is purely at inference time. This demonstrates that structured guidance provided as a prompt can improve small model performance without fine-tuning.

Constrained decoding masks illegal tokens at each step of the generation process \cite{willard2023guided}. This guarantees the output conforms to a specified grammar. It operates at decode time and limits the output distribution directly. Like an answer structure, constrained decoding illustrates that the distribution of a prediction can be sharpened with a structural constraint.

Coupled with an effective structural form provided at inference time, these elements bridge the gap between generalization and model size.

\section{Architecture}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Overview}

Our proposed solution is an inference-time pipeline consisting of two stages. The first stage consists of a classifier trained to map a question to an answer structure type from a predefined taxonomy. The second stage uses the classified structure to enhance the question prompt to a generator model. The pipeline is designed to enrich the prompt, allowing the generator to produce output through existing mechanisms. Any decoder-style LLM can serve as the generator. The answer structures are static, defined before inference.

\subsection{The Taxonomy}

The taxonomy defined in this paper serves as both the classification target and the index of the prompt scaffolds. The taxonomy contains 10 classes of answer structure, each representing a distinct relational role the answer plays relative to the question. Each class maps to a unique answer structure consisting of a set of labeled dimensions. Classes are defined by the structural form of the answer, not by surface features of the question such as the interrogative. The set is static and predefined prior to inference. The taxonomy presented here is scoped to the experimental dataset (\S4) and the process to derive it is outlined in \S4.3.

The 10 classes are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Object Identification} --- the answer names or identifies a concrete object, entity, document, technology, or substance.
  \item \textbf{Concept/Person Identification} --- the answer identifies an abstract concept, classification, or person by a defining property.
  \item \textbf{Multipart Identification} --- the answer requires identifying multiple distinct components.
  \item \textbf{Quantitative} --- the answer is a number: a count, measurement, proportion, or recorded statistic.
  \item \textbf{Temporal} --- the answer is a time expression: a date, year, era, duration, or range.
  \item \textbf{Place} --- the answer is a geographic location or spatial reference.
  \item \textbf{Agent} --- the answer is who or what performed an action.
  \item \textbf{Descriptive} --- the answer describes a quality, property, or characteristic of something.
  \item \textbf{Causal} --- the answer explains why something happened: the cause, reason, or enabling mechanism.
  \item \textbf{Resultative} --- the answer is the outcome, effect, or product of an action or event.
\end{enumerate}

Each class maps to a schema that specifies the dimensions a valid answer must address. Every schema contains two common pre-filled fields: \texttt{answer\_type}, which indicates cardinality (single or multiple), and \texttt{answer}, which names the kind of answer expected. The remaining fields are class-specific and empty, defining the structural components the model must populate. For example, the Causal class maps to a schema containing the effect, the cause, and the mechanism linking them. The Agent class maps to a schema containing the agent, the action performed, and the object of that action. The schemas differ in both the number and nature of their dimensions, providing the generator with structurally distinct guidance per class.

\subsection{The Classifier}

The classifier component is a BERT encoder fine-tuned for sequence classification \cite{devlin2019bert}. Bidirectional encoding allows the classifier to attend to the full feature set of the input question simultaneously, enabling more reliable classification than a unidirectional model would achieve on the same task. The input is the raw question text and the output is a single label corresponding to one of the ten taxonomy classes defined in \S3.2. The accuracy of this component is critical to system performance. An incorrect classification delivers a mismatched scaffold to the generator. However, the classifier is not the focal contribution of this paper. Its accuracy is evaluated as a system validity check, and the random structure condition (\S4) tests whether classification quality matters empirically.

\subsection{Scaffold Construction}

After classification, the framework constructs a scaffold at inference time as input to the generator. The purpose of the scaffold is to provide a structure that joins associated dimensional tokens into a single prompt. These tokens constrain attention and narrow the prediction space before generation begins (as established in \S2.8). Each scaffold consists of up to three components: the answer structure schema corresponding to the classification label, the input question, and an optional associated passage providing source context. The framework is configurable to allow for experimentation. Depending on conditions, the answer structure and passage can each be included or excluded. The question is always provided. Experimental configurations are detailed in \S4.

\subsection{The Generator}

The generator can be any small decoder-style LLM. It receives a scaffold as its prompt and produces a free-form answer. No fine-tuning or weight modification is applied to the generator as part of the framework. The answer structure operates entirely through in-context learning.

\section{Methodology}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Research Questions}

This work evaluates the framework against four research questions. Each maps to a comparison between experimental conditions (\S4.5).

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Does a matched answer structure improve accuracy on extractive QA?
  \item Does the specific structure matter, or does any structure help equally?
  \item Does the structure contribute independently of source context?
  \item Is the improvement consistent across model sizes?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Dataset}

The dataset used in this paper is the Stanford Question Answering Dataset (SQuAD) \cite{rajpurkar2016squad}. SQuAD consists of extractive question answering over Wikipedia passages and is a widely used benchmark for automated QA evaluation. Question answering as a task format spans many structural types such as factual, causal, temporal, and descriptive. One format covers many distinct answer forms, making it suitable for empirical derivation of a taxonomy. Gold-standard span answers further enable automated scoring via Exact Match and F1 without requiring human judgment. SQuAD also provides a source passage from which the answer is extracted. The passage serves as the context variable in our experimental design. It can be included or excluded, allowing us to verify whether the answer structure contributes independently of source context (RQ3). This constrained scope allows us to isolate the structural variable and demonstrate the concept. Extension to other domains requires only re-derivation of the taxonomy.

\subsection{Taxonomy Derivation}

As described in \S3, the taxonomy was derived empirically from the SQuAD dataset and is not based on existing literature. Each question was classified initially by four features: the interrogative, cardinality, the entity type, and the grammatical form of the answer. The initial four features proved insufficient. Questions with identical surface features often required different answer forms. A fifth feature, the relational context between the answer and the question, was added to capture the role the answer plays. For example, ``Who founded X?'' and ``Who was impacted by X?'' share the same interrogative and entity type but require different answer structures. The first identifies an agent, the second a resultative.

Using this five-feature model, all 5,928 questions in the SQuAD development set were classified using an LLM annotator. Each question was classified independently using a structured prompt specifying feature definitions and valid values. The consolidation step served as implicit validation. Incoherent labels would not produce structurally meaningful clusters. The resulting output produced 254 unique structural combinations, 67 of which contained ten or more questions.

The 67 classes were further reviewed and reduced based on two criteria: the structural distinctiveness of the resulting answer schema and class balance for classifier training. Classes that mapped to identical schemas were merged. Classes too small to train on were absorbed into their nearest structural neighbor. This produced 10 final classes (\S3.2), each producing a unique schema and sufficiently represented for training, with a largest-to-smallest class ratio of approximately 3:1. For example, temporal point and temporal duration both require the same dimensions (event, time expression, temporal type), differing only in content, not in guidance to the model. This taxonomy is scoped to SQuAD and is not claimed to be universal. It serves the purpose of demonstrating that a taxonomy of answer structures can be derived and operationalized.

\subsection{Classifier Training}

The classifier in the proposed architecture is BERT \cite{devlin2019bert}, fine-tuned for single-label classification over the 10 taxonomy classes. The training data would consist of the 5,928 labeled questions from \S4.3. Each question's five-feature label maps to its corresponding final class as determined during consolidation. A question labeled (who/singular/person/noun/agent) maps to the Agent class. The dataset would be split 80/20 into training and validation sets, stratified by class to preserve label distribution. The planned training configuration is 4 epochs with a learning rate of 2e-5 using the Adam optimizer, a batch size of 32, and a maximum sequence length of 128 tokens, with cross-entropy loss weighted by inverse class frequency to account for class imbalance.

\subsection{Experimental Conditions}

Eight experimental conditions are evaluated in this paper. Each condition provides the question and some combination of an answer structure and a source passage, either or neither. The conditions are as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Question only
  \item Question + passage
  \item Question + matched structure
  \item Question + passage + matched structure
  \item Question + random structure
  \item Question + random structure + passage
  \item Question + classified structure
  \item Question + classified structure + passage
\end{enumerate}

The matched structure is a direct match to the question based on the classification dataset. The random structure is assigned from the nine classes that do not match the given question. The classified structure is the label produced by the trained BERT model from \S4.4. Conditions 7--8 depend on the scaffold providing measurable improvement in conditions 3--4. If the oracle scaffold (matched structure) does not improve performance over baseline, classifier accuracy is irrelevant and conditions 7--8 are not executed. All conditions use identical decoding parameters (temperature and seed) to ensure differences in output are attributable only to the scaffold configuration.

\subsection{Models Evaluated}

Six open-source decoder models spanning two parameter tiers are evaluated. The small tier ($\leq$3.8B) includes four architecturally diverse models: Llama 3.2 1B (1.3B parameters), Gemma 2 2B (2.6B), Llama 3.2 3B (3.2B), and Phi-3 Mini (3.8B). The medium tier includes Llama 3 8B (8.0B) and Qwen 3 8B (8.2B), providing a same-scale cross-architecture comparison. All models are served locally via Ollama in their default quantized form. No fine-tuning is applied to the generator. Each model is evaluated on conditions 1--6 using a fixed stratified sample of 1,000 questions (class-proportional) drawn from the SQuAD dev set. Temperature is set to 0 for deterministic outputs. Response verbosity (token count) is recorded alongside accuracy metrics. Qwen 3 8B is a reasoning model that generates an intermediate reasoning trace by default; this was disabled so that it answers directly, on the same footing as the other instruction-tuned models.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Generator performance is measured by Exact Match (EM) and token-level F1, both standard metrics for SQuAD and directly comparable to published baselines. Results are reported as deltas between conditions: matched structure versus baseline (RQ1), random structure versus matched versus baseline (RQ2), structure-only versus baseline versus combined (RQ3), and all deltas compared across model sizes (RQ4). Classifier accuracy and per-class confusion are reported separately as a system validity check.

Because the generator produces free-form text rather than extractive spans, a raw EM comparison between the full model output and the gold answer would systematically undercount correct responses (e.g., ``Normandy is a region in France'' contains the correct answer ``France'' but fails string-level EM). We therefore apply \textbf{max-F1 substring extraction} as a post-processing step: for each prediction, we evaluate every contiguous token subsequence against each gold answer and select the span that maximizes token-level F1. EM is then computed on this extracted span. The search window is bounded at twice the length of the longest gold answer for efficiency, since the optimal span is unlikely to exceed this length. Both raw EM and extracted EM (EM\textsubscript{ext}) are reported; EM\textsubscript{ext} serves as the primary exact-match metric. This approach is prompt-agnostic---it does not alter experimental conditions or introduce confounding instruction tokens---and is a standard evaluation technique for generative QA systems where the model's response may embed the correct answer within surrounding text.

Token-level F1 is likewise reported in two forms: raw F1 (computed over the full model output) and extracted F1 (F1\textsubscript{ext}, computed on the extracted span). Raw F1 penalizes verbose outputs even when the correct answer is present, since precision drops with every extraneous token. F1\textsubscript{ext} isolates answer quality from verbosity and is used as the primary F1 metric alongside EM\textsubscript{ext}. Mean prediction length (token count) is reported separately as an explicit verbosity measure.

The two metrics agree on every comparison that follows: no scaffold condition improves over its no-structure counterpart under either metric, and the matched structure outperforms the random one under both. \paragraph{Matched structure and accuracy (RQ1).}
Matched structure does not improve accuracy in any model. Without a passage, the matched structure (C3) is lower than the question-only baseline (C1) for all six models; with a passage, the combined condition (C4) is lower than the passage-only condition (C2) for all six. The structure effect without a passage (C3 $-$ C1) is negative in every case, ranging from $-0.026$ (Llama 3.2 3B) to $-0.098$ (Qwen 3 8B). EM\textsubscript{ext} shows the same pattern, with the drop from C2 to C4 reaching 0.060 on Gemma 2 2B.

\paragraph{Specificity of the structure (RQ2).}
The specific structure does matter, even though no structure condition beats baseline: the matched structure consistently outperforms a random one. With a passage, the matched condition (C4) exceeds the random condition (C6) for all six models, most clearly on Gemma 2 2B (0.865 versus 0.804) and Phi-3 Mini (0.808 versus 0.767). The model responds differently to the correct schema than to an incorrect one, which indicates that the classification captures a structurally meaningful property of the question rather than an arbitrary label.

\paragraph{Independence from source context (RQ3).}
The structure does not contribute independently of the passage. The passage-only condition (C2) is the strongest or joint-strongest condition for every model, with F1\textsubscript{ext} between 0.791 and 0.952 when the passage is present against 0.335 to 0.482 when it is absent. Adding structure on top of the passage (C4) never exceeds the passage alone. The structure neither adds to the passage nor stands in for it.

\paragraph{Consistency across model sizes (RQ4).}
No conclusive evidence of a size-dependent effect was found. In the main experiment (Table~\ref{tab:f1ext}) the structure penalty (C3 $-$ C1) is negative for every model, but its magnitude does not track model size: the two 8B models differ more from each other than either does from the 1B model. A size-dependent effect appears only in the exploratory flat and sentence formats (\S\ref{sec:exploratory}), where the penalty on Llama 3.2 1B ($-0.115$ and $-0.172$) is markedly larger than on Llama 3 8B ($-0.025$ and $-0.090$). This preliminary evidence points, if anything, toward smaller models being harmed more rather than less --- the opposite of the hypothesis --- but the main experiment does not corroborate a size trend, and the question is revisited in \S6.

\paragraph{Verbosity.}
The scaffold's effect on output length depends on whether a passage is present, and in neither case does the change correspond to improved accuracy. Without a passage, adding structure shortens outputs; on Llama 3 8B the mean prediction length falls from 70.6 tokens at baseline to 57.6 with matched structure. With a passage, the effect reverses and structure lengthens outputs, from 27.4 tokens to 38.0 on the same model. The scaffold perturbs the form of the answer in both directions without improving its content.

\subsection{Comparison with Reference Performance}

No directly comparable evaluation of significantly larger models exists for this setting. SQuAD 1.1 predates current large language models and is widely treated as saturated, and the extraction-based scoring used here (\S4.7) is not identical to the official metric, so a like-for-like comparison against larger models cannot be drawn from published results.

The available reference points are the original benchmark figures. Human performance on SQuAD 1.1 is reported at 82.3 EM and 91.2 F1 on the test set, and BERT-large, a 340M-parameter encoder fine-tuned specifically for the task, reaches 84.1 EM and 90.9 F1 on the development set \cite{devlin2019bert}. With the passage, the smaller models here reach these figures on exact match, from 80.1 on Llama 3.2 3B to 86.8 on Qwen 3 8B, without any task-specific fine-tuning. Exact match is the more reliable basis for this comparison, since F1\textsubscript{ext} applies a substring-extraction step (\S4.7) that the official metric does not, and the F1 values should therefore be read as indicative. Even read cautiously, several of the smaller models sit within the range of the reference systems.

Broader evaluations of large models on SQuAD have been conducted, most notably HELM \cite{liang2022helm}, which benchmarks models up to 530B parameters. These are not directly comparable to the results here: they adapt models through prompting rather than fine-tuning, and HELM itself cautions that its findings may not transfer to fine-tuned settings.

\subsection{Classifier}

The classifier described in \S4.4 was not trained or evaluated. Conditions 7--8 supply the generator with a classifier-predicted structure, and per \S4.6 they are contingent on the matched (oracle) scaffold improving over baseline in conditions 3--4. The matched structure represents the ceiling a classifier could reach: a perfect classifier would reproduce the matched condition exactly, and any classification error could only move the input away from it. Since the matched condition does not improve over baseline, no attainable classifier accuracy would change the outcome. The taxonomy itself retains value, as the matched-over-random result (RQ2) shows it captures a real structural signal. A classifier stage, however, cannot compensate for a scaffold that fails to help even when supplied perfectly.

\section{Discussion}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Principal Finding}

As stated above, the central hypothesis is not supported. Across every model and condition, a matched answer structure did not improve extractive QA accuracy, and in most cases reduced it. The result is nonetheless only partly negative, and the part that holds is the more consequential.

The hypothesis combined two claims: that small models benefit from external structural support, and that the support they need is an explicit description of the answer's form supplied at inference. The results uphold the structural claim and reject the specific form it took. When given the source passage, every model improves sharply over its question-only baseline in F1\textsubscript{ext}. Llama 3.2 1B rises from 0.335 to 0.791, Gemma 2 2B from 0.388 to 0.919, and Phi-3 Mini from 0.415 to 0.834, narrowing the gap with the larger models in the study. The answer-structure scaffold produces the opposite effect. Supplied without a passage, it falls below the question-only baseline for every model, dropping Llama 3.2 1B to 0.299 and Gemma 2 2B to 0.337. Added on top of the passage, it still reduces accuracy, from 0.919 to 0.865 on Gemma 2 2B and from 0.930 to 0.915 on Llama 3 8B.

The passage closes the gap; the scaffold does not, indicating that what the small model lacks is not a description of the answer's shape but the structure already present in well-formed prose, which is sufficient to bring these models close to the reference performance discussed in \S5.2.

\subsection{Structure Matters, but Imposed Structure Interferes}

That the scaffold failed does not mean the model is indifferent to structure. A matched schema outperforms a random one in every model when a passage is present, for example 0.865 against 0.804 on Gemma 2 2B or 0.808 against 0.767 on Phi-3 Mini. The model responds to the correctness of the structure it is given. Yet the passage alone remains the strongest condition throughout, and no schema improves on it. Structure matters, but the explicit schema is not the way to supply it.

This points to the passage already supplying what the scaffold was meant to provide. A well-formed passage is drawn from the same distribution the model was trained on, and its organization aligns with the structural patterns the model encodes internally. As argued in \S2.2, those patterns are held as statistical, non-deterministic representations rather than explicit rules. An answer-structure scaffold supplies structure of the opposite kind: explicit, deterministic, and external to the representation the model already holds. For the question of the country in which Normandy is located, the passage states in prose that Normandy is a region in France, while the matched scaffold restates the target as the schema \texttt{\{answer: location, location: "", relation: ""\}}. The passage gives the answer in a form the model reads directly; the scaffold gives a template it must first map onto the same content. A matched schema aligns with the passage more closely than a random one, which is why it scores higher, but both add an unfamiliar second description of an answer the prose already carries.

This intersection was not tested directly. The experiments measured accuracy across conditions, not how the model processes prose and schema internally, so the explanation is not a direct result. It rests on two findings the study does establish: the passage is the strongest input, and an explicit schema reduces accuracy even when correct. Together with the account of structural encoding in \S2.2, these indicate that a natural passage and an explicit answer structure are different and competing forms of structure, and that this difference is the most likely reason the scaffold did not help.

\subsection{Understood Structure versus Imposed Structure}

If the passage helps because the model already understands its structure, then a model with weaker structural understanding should benefit less from structure of any kind. The runs in \S5.3, which vary prompt format and training separately, allow this to be examined.

Removing the chat template from the instruction-tuned model leaves its accuracy on the passage largely unchanged, at 0.930 versus 0.943. Removing instruction tuning lowers it sharply, to 0.776 under the same raw prompt. The model's ability to use the passage rests on its training, not on the prompt format, and the base model, lacking that training, reads the same inputs less effectively across every condition.

The scaffold fails in all three configurations. The instruction-tuned model in raw completion mode, stripped of its chat template, still loses accuracy when the matched schema is added to the passage, from 0.943 to 0.811. Its failure cannot be attributed to the extra framing a schema adds to a templated prompt, since the effect persists once the template is removed. The imposed structure itself is the problem.

Yet the two models do not fail in the same way. The instruction-tuned model scores the matched schema above the random one with or without its template; the base model does the opposite, scoring the random schema higher. The sensitivity that produced the matched-over-random result is thus acquired through instruction tuning rather than supplied by the schema. Structure can assist the model, but only once it has been trained to interpret it.

\subsection{The Taxonomy Captures a Real Signal}

The hypothesis of this paper rested on an enabling assumption: that a finite set of answer-structure types exists and is distinguishable to the model (\S2.7). The results support it. In the matched-versus-random comparison, the matched structure consistently scores higher. The model responds to whether the structure fits the question rather than to the presence of a structure alone. If the taxonomy assigned labels arbitrarily, the matched and random conditions would perform the same, but they do not.

The derived taxonomy therefore captures a genuine property of the questions. This part of the hypothesis holds even though the scaffold built on it did not. A taxonomy that produces a real structural signal can be derived. The difficulty lies in delivery, not in the taxonomy itself. Prompting with an imposed structure alone does not put the signal in a form the model can use.

\subsection{Model Size}

The results show no clear relationship between model size and the scaffold's effect. The structure penalty is negative for every model, but its magnitude does not track parameter count: the two 8B models show the largest and among the smallest penalties respectively. The passage, by contrast, improves every model substantially regardless of size.

This indicates that the scaffold's failure is not a matter of capacity that a larger model would overcome, at least among the models tested here. If size were the limiting factor, the penalty would shrink as models grew larger. It did not. That every model, from smallest to largest, is harmed by the scaffold and helped by the passage suggests this is a general property of prompt-supplied structure rather than an artifact of any particular model. This also runs counter to the original motivation for the scaffold, that smaller models, being more limited, would benefit most from external structure.

The models tested span only 1B to 8B parameters, and the largest current models were not evaluated. The conclusion therefore extends no further than this range; a wider one would be needed to characterize the relationship, if any, between size and structural guidance.

\subsection{Toward Structure the Model Can Use}

The scaffold we proposed was the wrong way to supply structure, but this does not mean the hypothesis behind it was wrong. The failure was not in the structure itself but in the model's ability to read it. An explicit schema reduced accuracy even when it matched the question (\S6.2). It continued to do so once the prompt template was removed, which locates the failure in the model's handling of the structure rather than in how it was framed (\S6.3). Additionally, the taxonomy itself is not without merit. The matched-versus-random comparison offers evidence, if modest, that it captures a real distinction among questions (\S6.4). The structure carries information the model could use, but not in the form it was given. What must be addressed, then, is the disconnect between the model and that form. Two approaches might close it, though each is a direction to explore rather than a solution the results establish.

An inference-time approach, consistent with the original design (\S2.6), leaves the model unchanged and alters the encoding instead. The schema likely fails because its explicit, deterministic form does not match the implicit way the model encodes structure. A delivery closer to that form might succeed where the schema did not, for instance expressing the answer structure in prose rather than as a rigid schema, so that it resembles the input the model already reads well. What form this should take is unresolved, however, and no clear evidence points to a particular one.

A training approach departs from the inference-time design and modifies the model itself. Rather than supplying the schema at inference and relying on the model to interpret it, the model would be trained on pairs of structured input and answer, learning to read the answer structure as it already reads prose. In effect the passage is replaced by the answer structure while the target output stays the same.

This shifts away from the position taken in \S2.3, that modifying a model's weights narrows it toward a specific task at the expense of general-task performance. It also gives up a property the original design was built for. An inference-time scaffold applies to any model without modification, but a trained model is tied to the weights it was trained on. The departure may nonetheless be justified. The distinction from ordinary fine-tuning is that the answer structure is content-independent. It describes the form of an answer, not a topic or a task. Training a model to read it would teach a general input format, much as instruction tuning teaches a model to read prompts rather than confining it to one subject. On this reasoning generalization need not be sacrificed, though whether it holds in practice remains to be shown. It would also require training data pairing structured inputs with correct answers, which is not trivial to produce.

Both approaches follow from the same finding: the taxonomy is not the problem, its delivery is. Whether the structure is brought closer to the model or the model closer to the structure, the goal is the same, to present the taxonomy in a form the model can act on. Both also rest on a more complete taxonomy than this study derived. Neither was tested here, and both remain directions for future work rather than conclusions this study establishes.

\subsection{A Note in Hindsight}

It is worth asking whether this outcome could have been anticipated, and in principle it might have been. The task is extractive, and the answer is a span within the passage, so with hindsight it is unsurprising that access to the passage would outweigh a description of the answer's form. That structure must be learned before it can be used also follows from the account of implicit encoding this work builds on (\S2.2). Both observations, however, are clearer in retrospect than they would have been in advance, and neither was obvious at the outset.

The extractive setting is central to this. The answer being present in the passage is precisely what allows content to dominate form, and this is a property of the task rather than a flaw in the design. SQuAD was chosen deliberately: it supplied the passages the framework required, a body of questions from which to derive the taxonomy, and gold answers against which to measure, all without the cost of building a dataset from scratch. The same feature that made it a practical choice is what made the passage so difficult to compete with.

This is raised to place the result, not to diminish it. Content is most likely to dominate form precisely in an extractive setting. A generative task, in which the model must compose rather than locate an answer, would test the role of structure more directly, and is left to future work.

% ============================================================
\section{Limitations}
% ============================================================

We acknowledge several limitations that bound the scope of these conclusions. The evaluation uses SQuAD, whose answers are short factual spans. Without the passage the model must generate the answer from memory rather than locate it, ideally still resulting in a brief span. Such answers have little internal structure, so a scaffold meant to lay out the form of an answer has little to organize. Whether the result holds for tasks with composite answers, where form carries more weight, remains untested. The model sample is also narrow. Six open-weight decoders between 1B and 8B span a limited range of scale, and no large-scale models were included. Whether the relationship between structure and capacity changes at that scale is therefore left open. The taxonomy was derived by hand from a single dataset and is not offered as definitive. A taxonomy built differently might capture a different signal. The study establishes that such a taxonomy can exist, not what its ideal form is. The final limitation we would like to address is an extraction-based F1 that recovers the best-matching span from the model output. Being more forgiving than the official SQuAD metric, it inflates the absolute numbers, so the figures here support comparison across conditions more than comparison with published results.

% ============================================================
\section{Future Work}
% ============================================================

A natural extension is to change how the taxonomy is delivered, since the results locate the failure in the delivery. As discussed in \S6.6, one approach is to change the encoding. Expressing the answer structure in prose rather than as a schema would give it a passage-like form, closer to what the model already reads. Another is to train the model on the structure directly, pairing structured inputs with answers so that the schema becomes a familiar input format rather than an imposed one.

A second direction is to revisit the definition of the taxonomy. It was derived here from a single extractive dataset. Building it over a wider range of tasks would show how far the idea generalizes. Those tasks might include multiple-choice answering, reasoning, and long-form generation. A broader taxonomy would also reveal whether one set of answer structures serves across tasks or each calls for its own. Evaluating the approach on these tasks would further test its continued feasibility and show which structures affect answering most.

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% ============================================================

This work asked whether a small language model benefits from an answer structure matched to the question before generation. Underlying this was the expectation that an explicit description of the answer's form would narrow the model's prediction space, and that a model constrained in capacity would benefit most. A taxonomy of answer structures was derived from SQuAD, and six models between 1B and 8B were evaluated across conditions supplying the matched structure, a mismatched structure, the source passage, and their combinations.

The structure did not improve accuracy. It failed to help any model and in most cases reduced performance, whereas the passage improved every model. What a small model lacks on this task is not a description of the answer's form but the structure already carried by a well-formed passage, one it has been pretrained to interpret.

The taxonomy nonetheless captures a real distinction. The matched structure consistently outperformed a mismatched one, indicating that the model responds to the correctness of the structure it is given. The limitation lies not in the structure or the taxonomy but in their delivery: an explicit schema competes with the structure the model already reads rather than reinforcing it, and this holds with or without the model's prompt template. Nor did model size prove decisive; the effect did not vary with scale in any consistent way across the models tested. Structure matters to a small model, as the hypothesis held, but an imposed schema is not an effective means of supplying it. The study contributes this distinction, a taxonomy shown to carry signal, and an account of why prompt-based delivery fails.

Whether such structure can improve generalization more broadly requires further study.
